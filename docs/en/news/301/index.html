Pulsars: stars that died to live – Hani Al-Dhulaie

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

An illustration of a neutron star (Reuters)

In the depths of the universe, among distant stars, lie stars that once blazed across the galaxy with white and blue light. They lived lives that were not very long, perhaps a few hundred million years or a little more, at surface temperatures above twenty thousand degrees and tens of millions of degrees inside. But after that they breathed their last, not quietly as humans and other living things die, but with a roar that shook the galaxy and which has only faded in its distant outskirts: these are the pulsating neutron stars.

Stars spend more than 90% of their lives stable, burning hydrogen fuel and turning it into helium. In the remainder of their lives they swell into giants, and the larger ones burn fuel by turning it into heavier and heavier elements until they reach iron, the most stable element in nature. The star can no longer fuse the nuclei of this element, so it collapses on itself in a terrifying explosion unlike any explosion we know on Earth.

This is the supernova explosion, in which the star's matter is torn into fragments that fill the universe with light a hundred times brighter than the whole galaxy. In it, iron no longer blocks the formation of heavy elements: the nuclei of lead, uranium, thorium and every element heavier than iron are formed, and all of them fly off with this nebula breaking away from the star, scattered across the sky as a huge cloud that keeps expanding even after its light fades, which had appeared as a new visiting star that was not on the star charts before.

The best known of these supernova explosions is the one observed in 1006 AD south of Libra, which was recorded in Arabic history books in Yemen and Egypt; another was seen in 1054 AD, known as the Chinese guest star in Taurus, and also mentioned in Arabic records. More recently, in 1987 a star exploded in the Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of our Milky Way, and lately two stars exploded in 2015 and 2016 in Sagittarius. Between these, several new stars were recorded, but they appeared faint on the far edges of the galaxy.

An image showing the remnant of a supernova explosion (AFP)

Stellar remnants
Such supernovae leave one of two kinds of stellar remnant. If the star's mass before the explosion was more than three times the mass of the Sun, the result is a black hole. The laws of physics give no precise description of it except that it is a dead region in spacetime where all physical laws vanish; any light that comes near cannot escape its grip but falls in forever, hence the name hole, and because it returns no light, it is described as black.

If the star's mass before the explosion was less than three solar masses and more than one and a half times the Sun's, the new product is a neutron star, sometimes a pulsar.

The word neutron comes from neutrons, the neutral particles inside the atomic nucleus. An atom consists of a nucleus containing positively charged protons and neutral neutrons, and around the nucleus, far from it, orbit negatively charged particles called electrons.

Just before it explodes as a supernova, the star begins to collapse on itself because of the enormous pressure on its core and the high temperature reaching hundreds of billions of degrees. The electrons can no longer orbit; the pressure forces them to merge with the positive protons inside the nuclei, neutralising their charge, so that the nuclei consist only of neutral neutrons, and from this the star takes its name.

Because the vast distances between the electrons and the nuclei are reduced to zero, matter shrinks to a tiny volume of very high density. To picture it, a small teaspoon (5 cubic centimetres) of neutron-star matter would weigh a hundred million tonnes, a number beyond imagination but calculated scientifically and mathematically. If our Sun were to die as a neutron star, its present diameter of 1.4 million kilometres would shrink to just twenty kilometres!

But is the neutron star really dead, or is there still some life in it? In 1967 the student Jocelyn Bell, while preparing her PhD at the University of Cambridge, picked up the first strange signal from space through a radio telescope made of a network of wires.

The riddles of the universe remain more and larger than human science and astronomers' observatories can count (Reuters)

A riddle
This discovery was the first real riddle from space. Scientists thought they had contacted "little green men" and that cosmic civilisations were trying to communicate with Earth, but the sources of these signals multiplied and had no explanation other than being a kind of strange star, especially as some of them sent strong X-rays in pulses over very short intervals, most no longer than a second, and some sent thirty pulses in a single second.

As observing technology advanced, scientists arrived at the best explanation for these bodies: very dense, rapidly spinning neutron stars facing Earth with their magnetic pole, around which electromagnetic waves circle, sent out in bursts whenever it turns towards Earth. They are thus dead stars but at the same time alive with these pulses.

Because pulsars, or neutron stars, are hidden rather than visible bodies, claims about their true nature are still not settled, just as with black holes, whose explanatory hypotheses still need more and more observational proof, despite their relatively wide acceptance in the scientific community.

The riddles of the universe remain more and larger than human science and astronomers' observatories can count. The Earth has not yet reached the size of an atom compared with the vastness of this universe, and the whole visible universe represents only 6% of the matter and energy in the cosmos; most of it is dark energy and dark matter, from which astronomers expect to learn a great deal in solving the most important cosmic riddles, foremost the Big Bang with which this universe began.
