International Asteroid Day 2019

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Prepared by Eng. Majed Abu Zahra

Today, Sunday 30 June 2019, the scientific community celebrates International Asteroid Day, an annual awareness campaign that aims to educate all the world's inhabitants to learn more about asteroids and what we can do to protect our planet, our families, communities and future generations.

International Asteroid Day coincides with the anniversary of the Tunguska catastrophe in Siberia, which occurred on 30 June 1908 when the largest asteroid to strike the Earth in modern history hit.

What are asteroids

Asteroids are airless rocks that orbit the Sun but are too small to be called planets. There are tens of thousands of these small bodies gathered in a main belt called the asteroid belt, located between Mars and Jupiter, and the asteroids that pass close to the Earth are called near-Earth objects.

Some call asteroids "minor planets". They are leftovers from the formation of the solar system about 4.6 billion years ago, and asteroid sizes range from the dwarf planet Ceres, previously classified as an asteroid, which is about 952 kilometres across, to small bodies under 1 kilometre, and the combined mass of all asteroids is less than the mass of the Earth's Moon.

At the dawn of the solar system's history, the formation of Jupiter prevented planetary bodies from forming in the void between Mars and Jupiter, so that the small bodies in that region collided with one another and broke into the asteroids we observe today.

The asteroid belt, or main belt, may contain millions of asteroids. Since asteroids have stayed as they were since their formation billions of years ago, studying them may reveal secrets about the beginning of our solar system.

Almost all asteroids are irregular in shape, and a few are round. They are usually full of holes and craters and orbit the Sun in elliptical orbits, in addition to rotating irregularly.

More than 150 asteroids have been shown to have one or two moons orbiting them, and there are also binary asteroids, of similar size, one orbiting the other, as well as triple asteroids.

The huge gravity of Jupiter and sometimes close approaches to Mars or another body change asteroids' orbits and push them from the main belt into space in several directions across the orbits of the planets.

Those asteroids have struck the Earth and other planets in the past and played an important role in changing the geological history of planets and in the evolution of life on Earth.

Scientists constantly monitor asteroids whose orbits cross the Earth's orbit, including through the scientific programme on near-Earth objects, which may pose a danger by striking the Earth's surface. Radar is highly valuable in discovering and monitoring the possibility of impact: their shape and size are determined and computer simulations are made of their orbit, speed and mass.

Scientists can increase their knowledge of the orbit of asteroids, their axial rotation, size, shape and concentration of metal. The United States is the only country that operates a survey and search programme to explore near-Earth objects.

List of asteroids

In the seventeenth century the astronomer Galileo Galilei was the first to see moons around another planet. In the twentieth century the spacecraft called (Galileo) discovered the first moon orbiting an asteroid. Most scientists imagined asteroids as lone mountains or rocks hurtling through space. But during the (Galileo) spacecraft's 1993 flyby of the asteroid (Ida), 19 miles across, it discovered that this asteroid has a small moon, one mile across, named (Dactyl), which was the first binary or double asteroid. Six years passed before another (asteroid–moon) system was found, and other discoveries followed:

In 1999 astronomers, using ground-based telescopes, discovered that the asteroid (Eugenia), 135 miles across, has a moon 8 miles across.
In 2000 it was discovered that the asteroid (Pulcova) has a moon about 9 miles across.
In 2001 scientists found that the asteroid (Sylvia) has a moon orbiting it and that the asteroid (Kalliope) has a moon orbiting it named (Linus).

Dozens of binary asteroid systems have been confirmed in the main asteroid belt and among near-Earth objects, some of which have a moon very close in size to the main asteroid. Some bodies beyond Neptune's orbit (trans-Neptunian objects) also have a binary system.

Facts you should know about asteroids

1 – If you gathered all the asteroids and compressed them into a sphere, it would be smaller than the Earth's Moon
2 – Asteroids orbit the Sun
3 – The length of a day on the asteroid "Ida", for example, is only about 4.6 hours, and it takes 4.8 Earth years to orbit the Sun.
4 – Most asteroids are solid, rocky and irregular in shape.
5 – Asteroids have no atmosphere.
6 – More than 150 asteroids are known to have a small moon around them and some have two. The first discovery of an (asteroid–moon) system was with the asteroid "Ida" and its moon "Dactyl" in 1993.
7 – Asteroids have no rings.
8 – Asteroids do not support life as we know it.

Flashes in the history of asteroids

1801: Giuseppe Piazzi discovered the first large asteroid (Ceres), orbiting between Mars and Jupiter
1898: Gustav Witt discovered the asteroid Eros, one of the largest near-Earth asteroids.
1991–1994: The (Galileo) spacecraft took the first close-up pictures of the asteroid (Gaspra) and discovered the first moon orbiting an asteroid (Ida), which was named (Dactyl).
1997–2000: The (NEAR Shoemaker) spacecraft flew near the asteroid (Mathilde), orbited the asteroid Eros and landed on its surface.
1998: The American space agency (NASA) established the Near-Earth Object Program to discover and track asteroids that may pose a danger to Earth
2006: The Japanese spacecraft (Hayabusa) became the first spacecraft to land on an asteroid's surface and return the first asteroid samples to Earth four years later.
2006: The asteroid (Ceres) was given a new classification, dwarf planet, but it keeps its status as the largest known asteroid.
2007: The (Dawn) spacecraft set out for the asteroid belt to study the asteroids Vesta and Ceres.
2008: The (Rosetta) spacecraft, on its way to study a comet in 2014, flew by and photographed the asteroid Steins.
2010: The Japanese probe "Hayabusa" returns to Earth after collecting samples from an asteroid.
2010: The probe "Rosetta" flies near the asteroid "Lutetia", and it is revealed to have resulted from violent events at the formation of the solar system.
2011–2012: The probe "Dawn" studies the asteroid Vesta, and "Dawn" is the first spacecraft to orbit an asteroid in the main asteroid belt, and it is still operating around the dwarf planet Ceres.
2013 – an asteroid 20 metres across exploded in the sky over the Russian city of Chelyabinsk and caused material damage and human injuries
2018 – an asteroid 4 metres across exploded in the sky over the Russian city of Lipetsk and caused no damage because of its small size.
