2018, a year of astronomical events – Hani Al-Dhulaie

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

2018 is an outstanding astronomical year because of the many events the Arab region and much of the world will see: two lunar eclipses, a transit of Mercury across the Sun, a historic close approach of Mars after an absence of 15 years, a meeting with Jupiter and Saturn, and a number of bright meteor showers.

On 3 January each year the annual astronomical events open with a meteor shower called the Quadrantids, after the constellation Quadrans Muralis, an astronomical instrument once used to measure angles of altitude. This constellation no longer exists among the constellations of the sky after the International Astronomical Union recognised the 88 constellations in 1928.

Because they are beautiful meteors, numbering between 100 and 120 an hour under clear skies, they have stayed at the top of the annual astronomical events with the same historic name.

In the same month, on its last day, the world will see three remarkable lunar events: first, the Moon will be a supermoon, at its closest to Earth, only 356,000 km away; second, it will be a "blue" moon, the second full moon in the same month; and finally it will pass through the Earth's shadow in space to give a lunar eclipse that will be partly visible from the Arab world.

But the Moon will rise already eclipsed, which will make things hard for observers, who will not find it easily because it is so dim. During a total lunar eclipse the Moon is lit by reddish light that leaks to it from the Sun through the Earth's atmosphere; without that, all lunar eclipses would be completely dark.

The Moon will rise eclipsed, which will make things hard for observers, who will not find it easily because of its dimness (Reuters)

For those who love rarely seen planets, Mercury will appear brilliant on the evening of mid-March after sunset, when it is at its farthest from the Sun, a phenomenon called greatest eastern elongation, when it will be 18.5 degrees from the Sun.

Because Mercury is close to the Sun and orbits inside Earth's orbit, seeing it is a challenge for astronomers. The Arabs gave Mercury its name ("Utarid") for its speed and the rarity of its appearance in the sky; famous scientists such as Galileo Galilei never saw Mercury in their lives.

About a month after that event, on the night of 22 April, another meteor shower becomes active, the Lyrids, named after the constellation Lyra, which contains the famous star Vega, a corrupted form of the word "al-Waqi'", after the "falling eagle", for the Arabs likened its stars to an eagle that folded its wings and fell.

In that shower observers will see twenty meteors an hour, followed a month later by the Eta Aquariids, named after Aquarius, on 6 May with sixty meteors an hour. These meteors are debris of the famous Halley's Comet, which visits Earth once every 76 years, last in 1986, and will return in 2061.

After that the great astronomical events begin with three beautiful, bright planets. First is Jupiter, giant of the solar system, which will reach its closest point to Earth on 9 May in a phenomenon called opposition, the planet rising as the Sun sets.

Jupiter, which never disappoints amateur astronomers, is the easiest object in the sky to observe after the Moon: its features and moons are easily seen even with small telescopes; it will remain visible for six consecutive months after that night.

Jupiter is among the easiest sky objects to observe after the Moon: its features and moons are easily seen even with small telescopes (Reuters)

Then, only two weeks after Jupiter, the Sun reaches its highest point in the sky with the summer solstice on 21 June. Two more weeks on, as the Earth goes round the Sun, it reaches its farthest point in its orbit, 152 million kilometres, on 4 July, even though it is summer; the seasons are determined not by the Sun's nearness or distance but by the angle at which its rays fall and the length of the day.

Second is Saturn, the ringed planet, when it comes to opposition on 27 June, rising at sunset, so that it and the Earth meet at their closest in their orbits around the Sun. With Saturn's appearance the sky is ready for observers, with Jupiter present and the Moon rising and setting.

Then comes the event of the year, the appearance of Mars, the Red Planet, returning after 15 years on the night of Friday 27 July, the meeting awaited since 2003. Amateur astronomers around the world look forward to it, and university observatories and Arab astronomical societies will open to the public to see the planet's features and its southern ice cap; amateur astronomers with modest telescopes will be able to make out some dark features on its surface and the white ice cap.

Earth and Mars meet every two years, but the great meeting recurs every 15 and 17 years in turn, when the distance between the two planets is only 57 million kilometres, a distance suitable for sending probes and spacecraft to the planet, just as happened at the 2003 meeting, when the American spacecraft Pathfinder reached the surface of Mars after seven months of flight, on 4 July 2004, so that the rover Sojourner it landed was the first rover to travel over the soil of the Red Planet in human history.

Amateur astronomers with modest telescopes will be able to make out some dark features on Mars's surface as well as the white ice cap (Reuters)

But the biggest surprise comes two hours after watching Mars: the people of the Arab world and around it have a date with the full Moon, which will enter the Earth's shadow in a very special event, a total lunar eclipse.

At exactly 9:25 pm Makkah time the Moon begins to enter the Earth's shadow, so that the Sun's white rays are blocked little by little until it disappears entirely and becomes a total eclipse at exactly 10:30 pm; the Moon will appear blood-red during that time. Then the Earth's shadow begins to recede at exactly 12:13 am as a partial eclipse, and the whole eclipse ends at exactly 1:19 am.

Then the astronomical events follow one another: on 12 August the summer meteors, the Perseid shower, become active at a rate of one hundred an hour. As the meteors of the summer holiday, watching them matters greatly to amateur astronomers, especially as this year they come with no Moon, whose light harms observation.

Four days after that, the brightest planet in the sky, Venus, reaches its highest point above the Sun just after sunset, taking first place in brilliance, and with it the four great bright planets gather in the sky: Venus, Jupiter, Mars and Saturn. If the crescent Moon is visible, it will be a pleasure to look at the sky then.

Saturn will be at opposition with the Earth on 27 June, rising at sunset, so that it and the Earth meet at their closest in their orbits around the Sun (Reuters)

Three meteor showers follow, one each month, starting in October: on 21 October the Orionids become active, named after Orion, at 25 meteors an hour. They are followed by the Leonids, named after Leo, the second most famous annual meteor shower, on 17 November, with a very low rate this year of 15 meteors an hour.

The third, the brightest and densest, is the Geminids, named after Gemini, active on 13 December at a high rate of 120 bright meteors an hour; this last will be the best of the three, as the Moon will be a crescent and will not affect observing.

Since only Neptune and Uranus remain among the planets of the solar system, these two planets will also appear in September and October respectively, visible with binoculars, or perhaps needing a small telescope once one knows the chart of their position.

On 21 December winter arrives with the winter solstice, with the Sun at its lowest in the sky of the northern hemisphere, bringing the longest night and shortest day.
