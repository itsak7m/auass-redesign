A beginner's guide to astronomy from scratch

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

I loved the night sky in my childhood. I spent long hours contemplating its bright stars and that faint smoky cloud lying in its south in summer, which I later learned was the centre of the galaxy. I was in the first or second year of middle school when my eyes fell on a simple book explaining where the stars are in the sky and how to recognise them. That book was my key into astronomy through its widest door, the door of the night sky.

Astronomy seems a complex subject tied to heavy physics and mathematics, and at some stage it really is. But isn't the best practice of astronomy to go out onto your roof or to a remote area on a clear night to contemplate that bright group of stars in the sky and learn a little about it? That, in my view, is the best start in astronomy.

As usual, I want to make clear that this is my own way into the wonderful world of the night sky, my own preferences and choices; all the sources listed here I have actually used or read. Learning astronomy can be divided into two stages: first, the night sky, and second, astrophysics. In each I will progress as usual from the simplest sources to the hardest.

Important note: you will need no prior knowledge of astronomy to start here.

Stage one: the night sky

Well, learning the night sky is like learning a new language: you must first learn its letters and words and then the rules that link the words. The words of the sky's language are the important concepts, which fall into two groups: the first is celestial coordinates such as the ecliptic, the celestial equator, the zenith, the nadir and so on, and the second is learning what celestial objects mean, such as nebula, double star, galaxy, open cluster and so on. Understanding how the sky turns is the grammar. Let us start gradually by giving you some simple sources explaining these topics:

1. The amateur astronomy course I personally presented with the Tahrir Academy will give you in just 45 minutes a very good start with the night sky; in simple everyday language with no equations or spherical geometry you will understand well the meanings of basic celestial terms such as constellation, ecliptic, north celestial pole and so on.

2. The YouTube channel Eye on the Sky offers three short episodes as a good introduction to the night sky.

These materials will help you get a simple grasp of the night sky's coordinates; in the last part of the article, stage two, the Khan and Crash Course courses will give you a good idea of the heavenly bodies. You now know that the night sky differs every day and that amateurs care about the changes that happen in it each month. Before going up to the roof, let us ask the most important question for you as a beginner.

What can I see with the naked eye?

The naked eye is the first way to learn the night sky, full of marvels. Over the year you can follow the following:

Stars

There is nothing finer than watching the sky on a clear night free of moonlight. To complete your enjoyment you should first understand the shape of the celestial sphere and important meanings such as constellation and brightness; these are simple pieces of information explained in the two earlier sources and will not take much of your time to learn. The International Astronomical Union divided the sky into 88 constellations, and anywhere in the world you can follow at least about half of them. Following the constellations and knowing their stars is the best start with the sky. After learning the maps you can begin with the brightest constellations and then the less bright. You can also learn directions and time by recognising the most important constellations for each month, and constellations like Ursa Major or Cassiopeia to find north.

The Moon

The Moon never gets boring. You can follow its growth over the lunar month and its changing phases, follow important phenomena such as solar and lunar eclipses, and notice the lunar maria. You can also follow lunar conjunctions with important stars or planets, which is often among the most enjoyable.

Planets

With continued observing you will be able to recognise the planets in the sky: Jupiter, Venus, Mars, Saturn and Mercury are all planets that shine in the sky, and you can follow their motion and their conjunctions with the Moon, with other stars and with each other. You can follow the planets' paths throughout the year relative to the Sun and Earth, learn their motion relative to the constellations, and notice the retrograde motion of some planets, such as Mars, relative to the stars.

The Sun

The Sun can be an important subject to follow with the naked eye. You can easily watch how the place of its rising and setting changes from your window over the four seasons, a little south, a little north or in the middle. You can also follow the length of the day and the Sun's altitude and learn the days of the spring and autumn equinoxes and the winter and summer solstices.

Meteor showers

One of the finest things to follow in the sky is a meteor shower. Many are surprised just to learn that meteors have times for falling. This happens when the Earth passes through debris left by a huge comet such as Swift–Tuttle or Halley: the Quadrantids in January, the Perseids in August and the Geminids in December, an astonishingly splendid sight. You can see more than 60 meteors an hour on the main nights of these showers, which are often announced.

Now you can go up onto your roof to take a look at the night sky. There are several tools that can help you strongly on your first nights with the stars:

1. Ida'at offers a monthly chart showing the most important events of the month, its bright stars and how to use star charts, as well as tips for amateurs and stories about stars. You can follow it regularly at the start of each month from here

2. The Hubblesite website offers a free monthly video explaining the most important events during the month, the ones you will follow with your own eyes; the video is simple and reliable.

3. You can download the Google Sky Map app from your smartphone's app store. It will help you recognise the night sky very easily even if it is your first time on the roof at night. The app uses the gyroscope, GPS and compass technology known on Android to make your roof the centre of the universe, and all you need is to turn your device to the sky and the shapes and names of all those stars or planets appear on your phone or tablet screen; all you need is to point your device at that bright star whose name you want to know. The app is free and very responsive, with a search engine to find the planet or star you want, and it guides you by pointing your hands in any direction.

4. The Sky & Telescope app for smartphones also gives you a daily guide to the most important astronomical events in a simple form you can follow.

5. The Stellarium program can be your first stop towards the sky. With it you will simply be able to recognise the places and names of the stars and the constellations. The program is free and open source. Open it and simply set the time and date and the location of your country and city, and enjoy following the night sky above your home; it will be a wonderful and very accurate simulation of the sky above your roof. The program gives you a huge database of 600,000 stars with a database of deep-sky objects, a great graphical interface and a realistic display of the Milky Way band and the atmosphere.

I will recommend some useful, enjoyable and simple books for beginners in series that I will present under the heading "Advanced additions" in a moment.

Important tips

1. Very good, you are now able to follow the stars in the night sky easily and practically; let me now give you some important tips:

2. Star charts and learning them are the main key to learning the night sky. Get the earlier sources and follow along with the night sky; this will improve your experience quickly.

3. Your roof is the first observatory you should start from, provided the lights are as dim as possible.

4. Start practising astronomy as a hobby, an enjoyable hobby that draws you a little out of the pressure and boredom of your life. A hobby is based on passion and love; the sky is romantic by nature, and approaching it with any other logic will limit your ability to learn all its marvels.

5. Practise watching the night sky daily, even for a few minutes.

6. The more constellations you know in the sky, the more your skill in it grows. Learning 25 constellations in the beginning is a very good achievement and puts your foot at the start of the ladder.

7. Rely strongly on Google and YouTube as research tools.

8. English-language sources are always better; in Arabic content I do not find many professional sources, but the book "Principles of Modern Astronomy" by Dr. Abdelaziz Bakri and the books of Dr. Abdelrahim Badr are good sources. You will find them on online shopping sites.

9. Join an astronomy group, even online, as that will help you continually learn of celestial events and practise your hobby with others who share your interest. There are many astronomy clubs in the Arab world: the Delta Amateur Astronomy Club and the Mostafa Mahmoud Astronomical Society in Egypt, the Jordanian Astronomical Society, the Emirates Astronomical Society, the Qatif Astronomy Society and so on; in every Arab country there is more than one astronomy club, and with a simple search you will find a club nearby.

10. Getting 10×50 binoculars is a very good start with the sky after the naked eye. You will easily find them on online shopping sites. Binoculars will help you see all the constellations with all their stars, and help you recognise many double stars, some bright nebulae and other celestial wonders, especially lunar craters.

11. Let the telescope be stage three after your eyes and binoculars. To learn how to buy your first telescope, you can read my earlier article: How do you buy your first telescope?

12. Be a little patient; it is natural for things to be hard at the beginning. The first FIFA match you ever played was surely the biggest defeat in history, as your friend may have decided to show off and beat you 25–1; it is natural for things to be harder at first… get used to it.

Advanced additions (optional)

Some references provide great support at a deeper level. If you want to keep observing the night sky and go deeper after getting a large telescope, I advise you to consider the following set of my own recommendations. The source of most of the books is Springer, the world-famous publisher, which opens its doors free of charge to Egypt only:

1. Patrick Moore's Practical Astronomy series for amateurs was always my favourite friend in my attempts to follow and understand the night sky by naked eye or telescope. The series has about 140 books, ranging from simple to professional, and covers every aspect of astronomy, a real treasure for every lover of this world. You can enter it from here

From it I recommend the following books for beginners:

Patrick Moore's Observer's Year: 366 Nights of the Universe (a daily sky guide!)

The Constellation Observing Atlas

From Casual Stargazer to Amateur Astronomer

Guidebook to the Constellations

Pattern asterisms

2. The Astronomer's Pocket Field Guide series, which has 8 volumes to help you observe enjoyably. The series is not necessarily for specialists but covers a number of wonders comprehensively, and is free to Egyptian users as part of the Egyptian Knowledge Bank. From this set I recommend to beginners Stargazing Program for Beginners.

3. For greater enjoyment I recommend the four-volume Deep Sky Companions by Stephen James O'Meara, Cambridge University Press, which you may find on the web with a short search, especially as a torrent file: 4 huge volumes treating many deep-sky wonders such as Caldwell and Messier objects, with a broad introduction to the physics of each, its location, how to reach it and how to observe and photograph it. The Patrick Moore series also offers many advanced books on observing deep-sky objects.

4. As a helper atlas I recommend the Pocket Sky Atlas or Sky Atlas 2000, or Deep Sky Hunter for a more advanced level; I use the last personally, designed by Michael Vlasov, offered free with several fine, organised and accurate lists; it can be printed on A3 paper and used to contemplate the sky with an 8-inch telescope.

5. For beginners with binoculars I recommend the Tackey atlas, simple and printable on A3 paper, free from here.

6. As for lunar atlases, there are several simple options: Antonin Rukl's atlas; the Photographic Atlas of the Moon, which explains Moon details day by day through the lunar month; and The Six-Inch Lunar Atlas from the Astronomer's Pocket Field Guide series mentioned a moment ago as a free source in Egypt. The Hatfield Lunar Atlas from Springer is also a good start for beginners; following the Moon through a medium telescope with a delightful lunar atlas is an unprecedented pleasure.

Stage two: astrophysics

After contemplating the night sky you will surely want to know that world more deeply. It is not just about observing a nebula thrown far away in the vast space; at some point you will strongly want answers to questions like: how do stars evolve? How do they form? What are their types? How do we recognise their types? How do those galaxies form? How did the universe come to be? Gradually a clear passion will develop in you for the physics of the universe and what is in it. Let us learn a little about that, starting as usual with the simplest sources:

1- The "Introduction to Astronomy" course given by the Crash Course channel over 47 short lectures of 10 minutes by the wonderful Phil Plait is the best possible introduction to astronomy; the course is easy and its topics are carefully chosen, and it will support your content on the components of the night sky, from nebulae and galaxies to star clusters.

2- Salman Khan offers a wonderful introduction, without equations, to astronomy and cosmology with introductions to geology and the environment. The course, in 85 short lectures of about 10 minutes each, gives a very good introduction to the shape of the universe and distances in it, then an extremely wonderful introduction to cosmology, the Big Bang, stars, their types and evolution and so on.

These introductions will give you a good chance to learn the physics of the sky without equations. You can also add either of these two books:

  1. Astronomy for Dummies – Stephen P. Maran
  2. Idiot's Guide to Astronomy – C. dePree, A. Axelrod

Each presents a very good approach to astronomy while avoiding equations entirely, beginning with introductions to the shape of the sky and our place in the universe, through a simple, long introduction to nebulae, galaxies and the evolution and formation of stars, then the famous theories in cosmology, relativity, quantum and so on, even strings.

Add to them also Astronomy Demystified by Stan Gibilisco, the best and largest of them, which sets out a complete curriculum you can rely on for semi-serious study.

For a slightly more advanced level, I strongly recommend Michael Bizan's YouTube channel, which explains all the basics of astronomy simply and at an advanced level with simple equations and a large number of solved problems. The problem with the astronomy curriculum on this channel is that it is not in one playlist; you will need some effort to extract the astronomy lists from the channel, from here.

For a more advanced level, I recommend two courses, the best on the web in astronomy

First: the astronomy course from Yale University, given in 25 lectures of one hour each. The course needs only high-school level to enter, is simple, not advanced, for first-year university students, and discusses 3 main topics beside the basic astronomy topics: planets beyond the solar system, black holes and dark energy. From here.

Second: in my opinion the best and most important possible university course in astronomy, for first-year students, comprehensive, covering all branches of astronomy beginning with observational astronomy, then the basic rules of Newtonian mechanics and important parts of physics to support astronomy, then the physics of the solar system, then the physics of stars, then cosmology, galaxies and relativity. The course is free of calculus and uses simple mathematics, with more than wonderful explanation by Dr. Ronen Plesser of Duke University; I advise everyone to take it. Sadly Coursera deleted the course, but you will find its complete material easily in a number of torrent files on the big sites as "Introduction to astronomy – Coursera", and someone kindly gathered the whole course material on his channel, from here.

I hear many questions about a reference in astronomy as famous as Serway in physics. In truth there is no single well-known general book in astronomy covering all details, only a number of suggestions; the most famous references in the field are often more specialised, such as Extragalactic Astronomy and Cosmology by Peter Schneider. My own reliance was always on courses, but I did some research and found suitable recommendations:

1. Astronomy Today, Chaisson, Eric.

2. Astrophysics for Physicists, by Arnab Rai Choudhuri.

3. An Introduction to Modern Astrophysics, by Bradley W. Carroll & Dale A. Ostlie (the most famous of them).

4. Introductory Astronomy and Astrophysics, by Michael Zeilik & Stephen A. Gregory.

5. Astrophysics Is Easy – Michael Inglis, a simplified introduction for amateurs from the Patrick Moore group.

6. Essential Astrophysics – Kenneth R. Lang, available free from Springer to all Egypt, suitable for university level.

You are now fully ready to dive into astronomy as you like and choose any branch to study more deeply. Personally I would like to make progress in cosmology or the study of nebulae; for you space will be open: planetary science, telescopes and their types and techniques, the study of galaxies, black holes, dark matter and so on. Choose what you love. Finally I would like to make clear that making any progress in astronomy will force you to learn some basics of physics and mathematics, so you may want to go back to an earlier article of mine titled Learn physics from scratch, in the summer, and you will also need to read some of the advice I gave there.

In more than 2,500 words I have given you a large number of suggestions and starting lines. You do not have to learn all this together; all you need is to pick the suggestion that suits you best and start from it. Focusing on one task at a time is the secret of success.

Yes, the night sky can change your life. Astronomical knowledge, even in its simplest form, can give your daily routine a kind of smoothness, patience and a measure of scientific thinking. Watching the sky at night and contemplating that great expanse is certainly a fierce break from the huge crowding of thoughts, people and work that we pass through every day without pause. The sky still has a role in your life, can still offer some of the enjoyment you can try in practice with your own eyes, and then you will wake the next day more aware of yourself and of the triviality of everything you may face in this difficult world compared with this unfathomable universe. It is a kind of peace that is sweet in company, a kind of calm you seek; from (your own roof) at night under a clear sky is the best possible resort. Following the night sky and learning a little about it can make your next day better.
