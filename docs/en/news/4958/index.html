Jeddah Astronomical Society: a total solar eclipse not visible from Saudi Arabia next Tuesday

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

The President of the Jeddah Astronomical Society, Eng. Majed Abu Zahra, reported that on Tuesday 29 Shawwal 1440, corresponding to 2 July 2019 (according to the Umm al-Qura calendar), the Earth sees a total solar eclipse visible from parts of the southern Pacific Ocean, central Chile and central Argentina, while it will be seen only partially in most of the southern Pacific and in Ecuador, Brazil, Uruguay and Paraguay.

All phases of the eclipse, from its beginning to its end, will take 4 hours and 55 minutes, and this event will not be visible in the skies of Saudi Arabia or the Arab world.

A total solar eclipse occurs when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up in a straight line, with the Moon passing directly between the Earth and the Sun. This event is relatively rare because the Moon's orbit around the Earth is tilted relative to the Earth's orbit around the Sun, and when those three bodies happen to line up, the Moon covers the Sun's disc and all regions that fall within the Moon's shadow will see a total eclipse.

During this eclipse the Sun's apparent size will be 1.6% smaller than average, while the Moon is two days from perigee, the closest point in its orbit to the Earth, which makes its apparent size rather large.

At the beginning and end of the eclipse the Moon's apparent size will be 3.3% larger than average, so it will cover the whole Sun, making this eclipse total.

In general the partial solar eclipse will begin across the globe over the Pacific Ocean at 7:55 pm Saudi time (4:55 pm GMT), when the Moon starts to move in front of the Sun's disc.

The first location will see the total solar eclipse over the Pacific Ocean at 9:01 pm Saudi time (6:01 pm GMT).

The path of the total eclipse, as it moves over the Pacific, will cross near the (Pitcairn) Islands at 9:28 pm Saudi time (6:28 pm GMT), where the width of the total eclipse is 169 kilometres at this location, and the total eclipse will last about 3 minutes 33 seconds. The islands will see a partial eclipse, with 97% of the Sun's disc covered.

After that the total solar eclipse reaches its greatest maximum over the Pacific Ocean at 10:22 pm Saudi time (7:22 pm GMT) and lasts 4 minutes 33 seconds, which makes it two minutes 40 seconds longer than the peak of the total eclipse that occurred two years earlier in 2017, and only people on boats, ships and aeroplanes will be able to take pictures during its longest duration.

The total eclipse will reach the mainland north of the city of (La Serena) in Chile at 11:39 pm Saudi time (8:39 pm GMT), in its final stages. Even so, the duration of totality will still be 2 minutes 34 seconds at the centre of the total eclipse path, which is 145 kilometres wide, and in the city of (La Serena) it lasts 2 minutes 15 seconds.

In addition, the (La Silla) observatory of the European Southern Observatory in the Atacama Desert in Chile is also on the path of the total eclipse. Although it lies north of the centre line of the eclipse path, the total eclipse will last 1 minute 51 seconds, from 11:39 pm to 11:41 pm Saudi time, at this location.

The total eclipse path will cross into Argentina, now moving fast, and pass north of the city of (Río Cuarto) after 11:42 pm Saudi time. The width of the eclipse path at this stage is about 136 kilometres, and totality will last 2 minutes 14 seconds at the centre of the eclipse path, and in the city of (Río Cuarto) 1 minute 59 seconds.

The eclipse path continues moving south-east and will end south of the city of (Buenos Aires) at sunset at 11:44 pm Saudi time (8:44 pm GMT). At this stage totality will last more than two minutes and the Sun will set totally eclipsed.

The last location on the Earth's surface will see the partial eclipse at 12:50 am after midnight Saudi time (9:50 pm GMT) and all phases of the eclipse end.

During the eclipse only observers within the path of the eclipse shadow can see many unique phenomena. They will see the "diamond ring" phenomenon, where sunlight appears as a lump at one edge of the Moon connected to a ring of light, which is the Sun's atmosphere shining around the Moon.

Because the Moon's surface is not perfectly smooth, an effect called "Baily's beads" can be seen just moments before totality, caused by sunlight rushing between the mountains, valleys and different terrain on the Moon's surface, and these grains or beads of light sparkle around the Moon's edge.

Finally the total solar eclipse occurs when the Sun's atmosphere, known as the "corona" or "halo", is seen. The observer's eye will then adjust to the new, lower level of light, and as the eye adjusts the solar corona becomes visible. This corona consists of extremely hot, electrically charged gas, plasma, at a temperature of 2 million degrees Celsius, extending millions of kilometres outward into space.

The solar corona will appear to stand out and grow, and this is only a type of optical illusion: the outer corona is fainter than the inner corona, and as the eye adapts to the darkness it becomes more sensitive to light, so the observer becomes able to see the faint parts of the outer corona, and so its size appears to increase. Even so, this optical effect is wonderful.

This is an ideal time to study it as a source of solar flares and the charged particles that stream from the Sun. So far little is known about it because of the difficulty of observing it under normal conditions, but a total solar eclipse gives an opportunity to observe it.

At the same time, on Earth it will be noticed that birds, for example, will stop their chirping during totality because they think night has fallen, and daytime flowers begin to close their petals and bees return to their hives. People may feel a slight drop in temperature when the main source of heat, "the Sun", is covered.

To watch a solar eclipse, eye protection must be used, such as special solar eclipse glasses that block more than 99.99% of sunlight, ultraviolet and infrared radiation. These glasses make the Sun appear as an orange or white disc in the dome of the sky, especially in the partial eclipse phase.

But when the total eclipse occurs, with the Sun's disc completely covered by the Moon, one can look directly with the naked eye alone, though it must be remembered that totality does not last long.

Scientifically the total solar eclipse phenomenon is of use in several fields. It was used in 1919 to prove the correctness of Einstein's general theory of relativity, which predicted that light would be deflected when passing near high-mass bodies such as the Sun, and this was confirmed.

In recent years total solar eclipses have been used to study the solar corona, whose temperature is millions of degrees Celsius, unlike the Sun's surface at only 5,500 degrees Celsius, for which no explanation exists so far. So this phenomenon is exploited to try to understand it, to try to learn the cause of the ejection of huge quantities of plasma into space through coronal mass ejections, to investigate the possibility of predicting solar flares and their cause, and to carry out several tests concerning the ionosphere layer, wireless communications and more.

It is worth noting that this total solar eclipse will be followed two weeks later (mid Dhu al-Qa'dah), on 16 July 2019, by a partial lunar eclipse that will be visible in all regions of Saudi Arabia and the Arab world.
