Everything that seems impossible is possible: the example of Hawking

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Stephen Hawking: a human example that perhaps will not be repeated often in human history; we were lucky to live in a time in which he lived

A few days ago the world woke to painful news: the death of the brilliant scientist and physicist "Stephen Hawking", who for more than 50 years lived his life in a wheelchair as a result of an incurable disease that wasted most of the muscles of his body until he lost the ability to speak.

But Hawking kept one part of his body working with unmatched efficiency, that mighty mind which travelled in the depths of the universe though his body sat in a wheelchair. Stephen Hawking is a human example that perhaps will not be repeated often in human history; we were lucky to live in a time in which he lived.

If the black hole is a physical singularity in nature, I do not exaggerate when I say that Hawking is a singularity in human history, before which history will stand for a long time.

Hawking may have been, for me and for many others, the ideal of physical brilliance and iron will. When I graduated years ago from the physics department of the Faculty of Science at Benha University in Egypt, Hawking was one of the shining names whose genius I tried to understand; I greatly enjoyed reading his books, which gave me a strong motive to try to learn the theories of that brilliant scientist. But when I looked at the details of his work I found that a student needs to learn whole sciences in physics and advanced mathematics to understand what Hawking writes about.

To read one of Hawking's papers you must be familiar with quantum field physics, general relativity, differential geometry, topology, statistical physics and other advanced sciences. In this article let us shed light on some of the contributions of that eminent scientist to physics.

Perhaps what Hawking is best known for is his original work in defining black hole radiation, known as "Hawking radiation", and this great discovery has an amusing story involving his student Jacob Bekenstein.

It begins when Bekenstein was discussing with his teacher, saying: we all know that randomness, or entropy, in the universe is increasing and cannot be reversed. Hawking replied: yes. Bekenstein said: but if you throw this cup of tea into the black hole, the entropy in the observable universe will decrease, so we must find a way to define entropy for the black hole.

Hawking replied that if you define an entropy for the black hole, then you must give it a temperature, radiation and all the thermal properties that go with the definition of entropy, which contradicts the black hole's property that it swallows everything and there is no way to learn any information about it, including its thermal properties.

Bekenstein worked to solve this dilemma in part and concluded that perhaps the surface area of the black hole is related to its randomness.

Hawking took up the matter in one of his papers, known as one of the most famous in physics of the last fifty years, in which he assumed that the vacuum next to the black hole contains infinite quantum fluctuations; because of the gravitational force of the black hole this leads to the creation of particles and antiparticles near its surface, one of which escapes carrying information about its temperature while the other falls in. These escaping particles are the building blocks of "Hawking radiation" from the black hole, and Hawking was thus the first to decode black holes and bring us physical information about their properties.

His work with Roger Penrose extended to forming the theories of physical singularities, in which they proved that every classical system must end in a singularity, which makes the merging of quantum physics and general relativity inevitable.

As a solution to that dilemma, Hawking offered proposals for a theory of everything, calling for merging all the forces of nature in one unified theory. Hawking spent his last years trying to solve the information loss paradox inside the black hole; his scientific motive was that this would amount to full knowledge of how the quantum world works in a strong gravitational system such as a black hole.

From this it is clear what a passionate seeker of truth in all its natural senses the man was. And on this path he triumphed over his illness with his brilliant mind, which made him immortal.

By cosmic coincidence he died on the anniversary of Albert Einstein's birth and was born on the anniversary of Galileo Galilei's death, as if the universe wanted to immortalise him in every possible way. When we remember the legends of science like Einstein and Galileo, we will remember a legend of another kind, who merged human challenge and scientific challenge in their finest form, leaving us a striking example that says: go on as you wish in nature, for the impossible has already been shattered from that wheelchair.
