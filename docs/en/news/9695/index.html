The New Horizons spacecraft to Pluto (1)

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Through NASA's ambitious project, the spacecraft New Horizons (NH) was launched on an unprecedented mission to explore one of the icy bodies at the far edge of our solar system: the dwarf planet Pluto and its moons. No spacecraft had ever travelled some 4.76 billion km to fly past a dwarf planet. The spacecraft is planned to continue its journey deep into the Kuiper Belt at the edge of our solar system and to visit one or two Kuiper Belt objects. A search is now under way for objects that match the energy stored on board, so that it can reach these distant icy worlds and fly close to them to complete a mission extended to 2020, by which time the spacecraft will be more than a billion miles beyond Pluto's orbit.

The International Astronomical Union redefined planets, classifying Pluto as a dwarf planet and part of the Kuiper Belt; it is the second largest after the dwarf planet Eris.

Pluto is a very different world: its diameter is less than half that of Mercury, sunlight takes 4 hours to reach it, and it is a thousand times darker than Earth. Its surface temperature ranges from −219 to −233 °C, and the surface consists of a mixture of rock, frozen water, methane and other volatile compounds.

These compounds may have remained essentially unchanged since the planets formed. Binzel, a member of the New Horizons science team, says this primordial material means the mission is "not just a journey through space, but also a journey through time".

Our knowledge of Pluto is limited and relatively unclear, because it had never been visited before. Scientists do not have clear images of it like those of the other planets of the solar system, so very little is known about its atmosphere, its surface topography and geology, and its chemical and gaseous composition.

Pluto in numbers

Discovered by American astronomer Clyde Tombaugh in 1930

Mean distance from the Sun: 5.9 billion km

Surface temperature: about −233 °C

Mass relative to Earth: 0.24

Day (Earth days): 6.4

Year (Earth years): 248

Diameter (km): 2,320

Closest distance to the Sun (perihelion): 29.657 astronomical units.

Farthest distance from the Sun (aphelion): 48.871 astronomical units.

Pluto's moons:

  1. Charon: 749 miles in diameter, discovered in 1978.
  2. Hydra (P1): 30–100 miles in diameter, discovered in 2005.
  3. Nix (P2): 25–80 miles in diameter, discovered in 2005.
  4. Kerberos (P4): 6–16 miles in diameter, discovered in 2011.
  5. Styx (P5): 6–15 miles in diameter, discovered in 2012.

The spacecraft was launched on 19 January 2006 at 19:00 UT on an Atlas V 551 rocket at a launch speed of 16.26 km/s, the fastest spacecraft launched at the time. The launch took place from Cape Canaveral, Florida, and the project cost a total of 700 million US dollars, for a ten-year journey covering about 4.76 billion km. It is due to make its closest approach to Pluto on 14 July this year and then continue to Kuiper Belt objects in 2016–2020.

The Kuiper Belt, at the edge of the solar system, extends 30 astronomical units beyond Neptune and consists of primitive bodies dating from the formation of the solar system's disc. Studying these bodies is key to solving the puzzle of the solar system's original structure, and determining their properties is essential to understanding the bigger picture of how the solar system is built.

These bodies were never exposed to the Sun's high temperatures, so they have remained in their natural state, preserved at very low temperatures, making them primitive samples from the formation of the solar system 4.6 billion years ago.

The mass of these bodies is ten times that of typical comets, and the Kuiper Belt is undoubtedly the source of most short- and intermediate-period comets.

Since 2011, ground-based observatories have discovered a number of possible targets for the NH mission, but because of their distance it is hard to reach them, as there may not be enough fuel for the spacecraft to get there.

The trajectory of New Horizons

The spacecraft took only a year to reach Jupiter, on 28 February 2007, covering 500 million miles in a single year, and passed within 1.4 million miles of the planet to use its gravity assist, gaining about 4 km/s in speed.

In 2007, as it approached Jupiter, the spacecraft's long-range telescopic camera (LORRI) took superb images of the volcano Tvashtar on the moon Io, measuring the plume of gas and volcanic vapour at 330 km high.

Pluto's long orbit carries it about 40 times farther from the Sun than the Earth, so it becomes less interesting to visit after 2020: its atmosphere may freeze completely and fall to the surface for decades, and there may be no other chance to visit it before 2230.

The distance the spacecraft will cover from Jupiter to Pluto is about 2.95 billion miles, taking about 8 years and 8 months.

The image shows Jupiter and its moon Io, taken by New Horizons

The spacecraft's hibernation during its journey:

In hibernation, most of the spacecraft's power is switched off except for the flight computer. This reduces operating costs, preserves the spacecraft's instruments, avoids failures of some systems during the long journey, and frees the ground stations' long-range antennas for other space missions. The instruments were put into hibernation mode, which allows low power to run only the flight computer and the electronic clock; the clock then automatically restarts the spacecraft at the set time, waking its instruments into active mode, and the spacecraft sends a radio signal to the ground station confirming that its instruments are running at full power at the pre-programmed time.

Hibernation periods during the journey (instruments switched off):

  • Since its launch in January 2006, the spacecraft has been put into hibernation several times, for a total of 1,873 days, about two thirds of the journey.
  • From mid-2007 to the end of 2014 there were 18 hibernation periods, ranging from 36 days at the shortest to 202 days at the longest.
  • Each year some instruments are switched on only two or three times to check certain systems and, if needed, to realign the spacecraft's navigation equipment.
  • On 25 August 2014 the spacecraft crossed Neptune's orbit, and its science instruments analysed the distribution of hydrogen around Neptune's orbit; the spacecraft was then put into hibernation for the last time, for 99 days.
  • Finally, on 6 December 2014, 162 million miles from Pluto, the spacecraft woke from hibernation as NASA had planned and its instruments switched on automatically; the spacecraft was then 2.9 billion miles from Earth. The confirmation signal was received at 9:30 pm US Eastern time, after taking 4 hours and 25 minutes to reach the ground station.

After waking in December, the spacecraft continues for six months on its course towards Pluto, to fly past it and then continue its mission to the Kuiper Belt.

The spacecraft's mission: to determine the composition of the atmosphere precisely and how it interacts with outer space, and to study how some gases may turn into escaping volatiles through their interaction with sunlight.

Pluto's composition: Pluto's core makes up about 70% of its diameter. The bright regions of the surface appear covered with nitrogen snow and smaller, solid amounts of methane, ethane and carbon monoxide, while the dark regions may consist of hydrocarbon molecules, according to recent observations by the Hubble Space Telescope.

The telescopic camera is used in navigation to locate Pluto and measure the distance between the spacecraft and Pluto precisely, so that the trajectory can be corrected. A gyroscope and a star tracker are also fitted to the spacecraft to set its position precisely.

The science instruments at each stage of the approach to Pluto:

First approach phase:

  • SWAP measures the rate at which the solar wind is lost from Pluto and its interaction;
  • PEPSSI measures the composition and density of the varying plasma;
  • LORRI will track the motion of Pluto and its moons.

Second approach phase:

In this phase, colour imaging will be used to search for moons and rings around Pluto. From this distance, 121 to 26 million km, the images will be sharper than those of the Hubble Space Telescope.

Third approach phase: with 21 days left before closest approach, this phase includes the best observations because of the proximity to Pluto, producing the best global maps of Pluto and its moon Charon. PEPSSI and SWAP will observe the bow shock, which forms when the solar wind collides with Pluto's magnetic environment, creating a curved wave; for example, the solar wind hitting our planet forms a shock where it meets the Earth's magnetic field at about 90,000 km. LEISA and Alice, an ultraviolet spectrometer, will search for light clouds or haze on Pluto to analyse its thin atmosphere.

Final approach phase: from 13 to 15 July, when the spacecraft reaches its closest point to Pluto, about 12,500 km. This phase includes the most important scientific observations by the instruments on New Horizons.

The science instruments at each stage of departure from Pluto:

First departure phase: from 15 July to 4 August, SWAP and PEPSSI will observe Pluto's magnetic tail and measure Pluto's temperature on its dark night side, and observations will also focus on the moons Nix and Hydra and the search for possible rings around Pluto.

Second departure phase: from 24 to 119 million km; no data are planned.

Third departure phase: from 25 August to January 2016, with no plans to operate the science instruments; this phase runs from 119 million km to 203 million km.

New Horizons' closest distances on 14 July will be:

  • 12,500 km from Pluto
  • 28,500 km from Charon
  • 22,000 km from Nix
  • 77,600 km from Hydra

The scientific goals of the mission:

  • To study the topography of Pluto and its moons
  • To map the composition of its surface and its temperatures
  • To examine the structure of Pluto's thin atmosphere by analysing its composition.
  • To search for new moons of Pluto and for possible rings around it
  • To learn whether Kuiper Belt objects are primordial remnants of the disc from which the planets formed, or fragments of larger bodies produced by repeated collisions in the outer solar system.
  • To determine the size distribution of Kuiper Belt objects, which in turn will help explain the nature of the comets we see in the inner solar system
  • New Horizons will reveal more about the properties of Kuiper Belt objects and how much they differ from one another.
  • Some theories hold that colliding Kuiper Belt objects were important sources of water and of the Earth's atmosphere, and that complex hydrocarbons helped provide the basic ingredients needed for life on Earth. Observing and studying some Kuiper Belt objects may shed more light on the history of our planet and the origin of life on it.

New Horizons may determine which scenario actually produced Charon: will Charon's overall composition and density be completely different from Pluto's, or is there some similarity pointing to an immediate formation for both? Detailed measurements of their sizes, their motion relative to each other and the composition of their surfaces should help resolve the question.

As for Pluto's two recently discovered moons, the most likely scenario is that they resulted from the same impact that created Charon, since they are relatively close to Pluto and orbit in the same plane and direction as Charon.

That idea, however, does not answer whether all the moons formed slowly from a disc or arose immediately after the impact. It also remains possible that the two new moons are separate bodies that Pluto captured at a different time.

Receiving and transmitting data from the spacecraft

The spacecraft carries a 2.1-metre dish antenna to receive signals from the ground stations and to transmit data from its science instruments at 2,000 bits per second. The Deep Space Network consists of three complexes spread around the world: one in California in North America, one in Spain in Europe and one in Australia in the southern hemisphere. All these stations support space communications for NASA and the European Space Agency, and each complex has a dish 70 metres in diameter.

Through the Deep Space Network in California, data are relayed from the Applied Physics Laboratory to the science centre in Colorado for scientific analysis and later archived by NASA in its Planetary Data System.

The spacecraft's power source: the radioisotope battery

Radioisotope thermoelectric generator

This is a battery that produces electric current from the heat generated by the radioactivity of suitable radioactive isotopes. It does not produce electricity through nuclear fission, as used in nuclear reactors, but through the natural radioactive decay of isotopes, and the two should not be confused. Radioisotope batteries are used in spacecraft sent to image and study the planets around us, such as Pioneer, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, Cassini and many others; they were first used by the US Navy in 1961.

When the spacecraft reaches Pluto, planetary scientists and amateur astronomers will eagerly await its discoveries and their results, which may overturn our ideas about the models and properties of dwarf planets and of the thousands of Kuiper Belt objects. The New Horizons mission will end in 2026.

These bodies, preserved in the frost of space far from the Sun, may be remnants of what existed when the solar system formed. As we study Pluto and its surroundings up close, we will see almost untouched remnants of the era of planet formation, which may hold a chemical and structural record that has been erased from the bodies we have studied so far in the inner solar system.
