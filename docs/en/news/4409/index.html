Science news · 3 May 2019

New study: Mercury has a solid core

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Prepared by Eng. Majed Abu Zahra

A scientific team, after analysing data from NASA's MESSENGER probe mission, revealed that they managed to solve one of the biggest secrets of the planet Mercury by discovering that it has a solid inner core. The new results were published in Geophysical Research Letters on 15 April 2019.

Mercury is the smallest planet in our solar system and the closest to the Sun. It does not resemble the Earth on the outside, but deep inside the story is different.

It has long been known that Mercury has a liquid metallic outer core like the Earth's, but planetary scientists did not know whether Mercury has a solid inner core like the Earth's.

Now, thanks to analysis of data from the MESSENGER probe mission, it turns out that Mercury's core is solid, and also about the same size as the Earth's inner core, even though Mercury itself is much smaller than the Earth.

The study indicates that the interior of the planet Mercury is still active because of the molten core that generates a weak magnetic field for the planet, and compared with the Earth, Mercury's interior has cooled faster than our planet's, so Mercury may help predict how the Earth's magnetic field will change as its core cools.

Mercury has always been considered a strange planet compared with the other rocky planets in our solar system: its core occupies about 85% of the volume of the whole planet, much more than the cores of any of the other rocky planets, including the Earth, and the reason is still unknown.

The important question here: how did the scientific team come to know whether Mercury has a solid or liquid inner core?

They used a lot of data from several observations collected by the MESSENGER probe, which orbited Mercury from 2011 to 2015. The planet's rotation information and gravity measurements provided the evidence needed to solve the puzzle of the planet's inner core.

Scientists looked for gravity anomalies in regions where there is an excess or deficit of mass, using radio data from the MESSENGER probe, and similar gravity anomalies have been found on the Moon.

The planet Mercury rotates about its axis very slowly compared with the Earth, with a day on Mercury lasting 88 Earth days. The changes observed earlier were enough to determine that the planet has a molten outer core, but this was not enough to learn the nature of the inner core, so gravity was analysed by looking at how the probe accelerated under the influence of Mercury's gravity, especially during low-altitude orbits, and then more precise measurements of the inner part of the core could be made.

The results showed that Mercury must have a large, solid inner core, estimated to be about 2,000 kilometres across, which means it makes up about half of Mercury's entire core, which is about 4,000 kilometres across.

The Earth's inner core is about 2,400 kilometres across, which occupies more than a third of this planet's whole core.

It took many different types of data to reach this result, and such solidification of the core shows how the deep part of Mercury is like that part of the Earth. So understanding the nature and evolution of the internal structure of other planets is essential to enhancing our knowledge of the Earth's future, and every new piece of information about our solar system helps us understand the universe.
