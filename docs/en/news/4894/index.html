Science news · 24 June 2019

Study: a huge coronal mass ejection detected from the star HR 9024

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Source: www.nature.com

Prepared by Eng. Majed Abu Zahra

Coronal mass ejections are known to be often associated with solar flares and are the most powerful magnetic phenomena occurring on the Sun. Now astronomers, using NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory, have detected the explosion of a huge coronal mass ejection from the active star HR 9024.

This is the first time a coronal mass ejection has been seen in a star other than the Sun, and the results, published in the journal Nature Astronomy, confirm that coronal ejections are produced in magnetically active stars and are relevant to stellar physics.

A coronal mass ejection is a giant cloud of solar plasma tied to magnetic field lines that is thrown out from the Sun over several hours.

Although the Sun's corona was observed during total solar eclipses thousands of years ago, the existence of the coronal mass ejection was not known until the space age.

Coronal mass ejections are often associated with solar flares and prominence eruptions but may also occur in the absence of either of these processes, and the frequency of coronal mass ejections varies with the sunspot cycle.

The technique used in this study depends on monitoring the speed of plasma during stellar flares.

This is because, by analogy with the solar environment, during a flare the plasma confined in the coronal loop where the flare occurs is expected first to move upward, then downward to reach the lower layers of the star's atmosphere.

Furthermore, an additional motion is also expected, always directed upward, since the coronal mass ejection is associated with the flare.

The science team used X-ray spectroscopy data from the Chandra observatory and analysed the flare detected from the star HR 9024, a giant star 455 light years away in the constellation Andromeda, about 10 times larger than the Sun and about 3 times its mass.

The results of the Chandra observations clearly show that during the flare very hot material (at temperatures between 10 and 25 million degrees Celsius) rises and then falls at speeds between (361,100 and 1,450,000 kilometres per hour). This is in complete agreement with the expected behaviour of material associated with a stellar flare.

This previously unknown result confirms that our understanding of the main phenomena occurring in flares needs updating. Scientists were not fully sure that the predictions could match such observations, because the prevailing understanding of flares is based almost entirely on observations of the solar environment, where the most extreme flares are a hundred thousand times less intense in emitted X-rays.

The most important point in this study: it was found that after the flare occurred, the plasma, at a temperature of 3.9 million degrees Celsius, rises from the star at a constant speed of about 300,000 kilometres per hour, and these data are exactly what one would expect for a coronal mass ejection associated with a stellar flare.

The Chandra observatory's data, in addition to the speed, made it possible to learn that the mass of the coronal mass ejection equals a billion billion pounds, about 10,000 times greater than the huge coronal mass ejections the Sun throws into interplanetary space. This is consistent with the idea that coronal mass ejections in active stars are a larger-scale version of solar coronal mass ejections.

It is worth noting that the observed speed of the coronal mass ejection is much lower than expected, which indicates that the magnetic field in active stars may be less efficient at accelerating coronal mass ejections than the Sun's magnetic field.
