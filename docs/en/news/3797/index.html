Science news · 2 March 2019

30 days without sunspots

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Prepared by Eng. Majed Abu Zahra

This morning, Saturday 2 March 2019, 30 days in a row will have passed with the Sun's disc completely free of spots. The last time this happened was in August 2008, when the Sun was deep in its minimum of activity, the deepest of the space age.

Currently a new solar minimum is forming that is likewise deep: so far this year, 2019, the Sun has been spotless 73% of the time, the same as in 2008.

The solar minimum phase is a natural part of the solar cycle, which occurs every 11 years, when the number of sunspots falls to "zero" and the dark cores that produce solar flares and coronal ejections disappear from the Sun's surface, leaving the Sun blank for a long period of time.

Solar minima have come and gone regularly since the discovery of the sunspot cycle in 1859.

However, solar minima are not all alike. The last was in 2008–2009, when observers were surprised by its depth and side effects: sunspot numbers fell to their lowest level in 100 years, the Sun became 0.1% dimmer, and the upper atmosphere of the Earth cooled and shrank, which allowed space junk (debris from space flights and old satellites) to pile up in orbits around the Earth, and the pressure of the solar wind dropped, matched by a rise in cosmic rays (the solar wind normally resists them). All this is happening again now.

As for the effects during this part of the solar cycle, there may be cosmic rays, as high-energy particles from deep space penetrate the inner part of our solar system more easily. These can be detected by NASA spacecraft and weather balloons, but they have no effect on people on the Earth's surface.

Solar activity is known to take many forms, but attention is usually focused on sunspots, whose dark cores are counted and spread over the measurement of the solar cycle.

In conclusion, with the end of February and the start of March without sunspots… welcome, solar minimum.
