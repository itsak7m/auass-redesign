Are parallel universes real? – Hani Al-Dhulaie / Al Jazeera Net

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

There is a clear difference between what some interpret as the concept of "the heaven and the heavens" in the Holy Qur'an and the concept of "the universe", a modern term the Arabs did not know before. The heaven is everything above, and the heavens are layers of unknown reality that may be a kind of the unseen. But the universe is everything material that is or will be, and in its simple definition includes stars, galaxies, planets and everything that can be observed, even the dark matter and dark energy that cause galaxies to move apart from one another in what is known as the expansion of the universe or of spacetime, which together make up more than 94% of the universe, though they have not yet been clearly detected. Going back to the famous physicist Albert Einstein, he defines the universe as all mass, energy, time or space. Time cannot be imagined without space, nor space without time passing over it. It is unbounded but finite. Its visible radius so far is 13.8 billion light years, but its calculated radius is many times more. The universe is governed by unified laws that do not differ or conflict anywhere in it: the mass of a hydrogen nucleus here is the same as the mass of a hydrogen nucleus at the other end of the universe, the charge of the electron is constant anywhere, and likewise the atomic number and mass of any element of the periodic table and its isotopes are fixed and do not change. Information travels by light, which also has a constant speed that never changes in space and vacuum. Since the universe's matter is one and its laws are one, the information travelling in it follows the same laws. But what if there were another universe besides ours? Would it be right to call it a universe? Perhaps, but the difficulty, perhaps the impossibility, would be in knowing how we could communicate with that universe; is that acceptable rationally or at least mathematically?

"The existence of another universe separate from ours requires a certain, truthful report, like reports of the unseen, for contact with it would not be possible if its cosmic laws and constants differed from those of our universe."

The secret is that the existence of another universe separate from ours requires a certain, truthful report, like reports of the unseen, for contact with it would not be possible if its cosmic laws and constants differed from those of our universe; a ray of light could not then carry us any news, because it would not be the right messenger to understand the language or nature of that universe. Not to mention another, bigger dilemma: according to Einstein's general theory of relativity, time and space were created together at the moment of the Big Bang with which our universe began about 13.8 billion years ago, and if everything we know or will ever know lies within the bounds of this universe, how can we, being of its nature and part of its law, observe anything outside it? The terms "outside" and "inside" are rejected by Big Bang theory, since they have no meaning before the universe and were created with it; inside and outside are parts of the universe itself, so we cannot imagine a universe as a ball, say, with another universe like another ball beside it, because the void between them would contradict the earlier concept. Yet scientists have no choice but to do so when speaking of multiple or parallel universes. Do the laws of physics forbid such universes? The idea of parallel universes goes back to 1954, when a physicist named Everett tried to solve the equations of quantum theory, especially those concerning the probabilities of quantum events (at the atomic level), known as the uncertainty principle, which says that when we study quantum matter (subatomic particles) we will observe it behaving in an unruly way, so that we cannot be sure of its nature or properties. For example, if we study the behaviour of photons of light we find that they sometimes behave as particles and sometimes as waves. Nor can one say which of two possibilities will occur for an event with a 50% chance: if we put two balls, one white and one black, in a bag and want to draw just one, the chance of each is equal and one cannot say beforehand which ball will be drawn. If the white one is drawn, say, fate is carried out, and the chance of white becomes 100% and of black 0%. But the physicist Everett, to get out of this predicament, supposed that this same person exists in another universe parallel to ours doing the same experiment at the same time but drew the black ball instead of the white, so both possibilities came true. If there are three possibilities there are three universes, if a hundred there are a hundred, to an endless number of parallel universes arising at the moment of choice. But those parallel universes are not accepted by reason (given the breadth of our understanding and logic), though it is supposed that reason does not grasp them, which sparked wide debate among scientists and raised questions with no answers, leaving the form in which the theory was presented thoroughly rejected, among them: when were these worlds or universes formed? How could they form so quickly? Or perhaps they have existed from the start? What is their relation to one another? Which is the original universe and which the dependent one? And other questions.

"Superstring theory says the smallest constituents of the universe's matter are not atoms, protons and quarks, but there are infinitely small things that are the basic building blocks of matter, strings vibrating in different ways, forming the matter we know around us of natural elements, earth, air, planets, stars and galaxies."

In the 1970s another theory appeared, more consistent than Everett's, superstring theory, which says the smallest constituents of the universe's matter are not atoms, protons and quarks, but there are infinitely small things that are the basic building blocks of matter, strings vibrating in different ways, forming the matter we know around us of natural elements, earth, air, planets, stars and galaxies. The theory adds that these strings live in a universe of nine dimensions, not only the three in which we live. So the worlds living in the other six dimensions, higher than ours, can see us and we cannot see them, perceive us and we do not perceive them. According to that theory there are universes parallel to ours, but we do not know whether they are inside our universe or outside it. But if the veils were lifted and these mathematically computed dimensions (for which there is not the slightest material or observational evidence to this day) became manifest to us, we would then be able to see those parallel universes and other worlds. Perhaps some go so far as to explain the worlds of the jinn and the angels in this way, as they exist in (dimensions) in which they perceive us and we do not perceive them, and they see us and we do not see them. In the 1980s the theoretical physicist Stephen Hawking and a physicist named James Hartle put forward a hypothesis that the Big Bang left parallel universes, like bubbles side by side, some of these universes resembling ours in laws and constituents and others differing from it in everything. But Hawking recently reconsidered this hypothesis and concluded that if there were an infinite number of universes with different physical laws in each, it would be impossible to determine the type of universe we live in from the observations cosmologists make. So before his death he discussed this problem with the Belgian physicist Thomas Hertog and, keeping the calculations of superstring theory, they came up with new mathematical models indicating that these parallel universes have the same physical laws. As a result our universe is like any other, and one can study the emergence of all the other parallel universes by studying our own. Does religion, for example, deny the existence of other universes and other people and seven layered heavens? Did the Qur'an mention texts that explicitly affirm or deny that? It seems the answer is no, knowing that many who delve into the interpretation of the verses on the beginning and end of creation think the Big Bang theory is explicitly mentioned in the Holy Qur'an, and it is not (in my opinion as a researcher): the verses of "joined entity" and "splitting" and the verses on the folding of the heaven have no relation to the origin or end of the universe, but indicate the origin of the Earth and our solar system, while the term "the heavens" remains an unseen whose interpretation we do not know to this day and which may be revealed to some who come after us by tens or hundreds of years. Besides that, the collapse of the universe under the name of the Big Crunch, the opposite of the Big Bang, requires the annihilation of all matter, including stars, planets, the Sun, the Earth and the sky, which is contrary to what the Holy Qur'an decrees when it mentions the Sun being wrapped up, the Earth and mountains being changed, the sky changing shape, the stars falling in disorder, and the planets scattering, all of which proves that these things remain visible; had they perished the Qur'an would not mention them in its repeated discussion of the events of the Hereafter. Theories of the creation of the universe remain mere hypotheses and purely mathematical models, some of which are borne out by successive observations, such as Big Bang theory, supported by three original and strong observations the theory predicted: the ratio of hydrogen to helium in the universe, 75%–25%; the moving apart of galaxies (discovered by Hubble in 1928 and found in the solutions of the Russian physicist Friedmann to Einstein's equations); and the initial temperature of the universe, equal to 270 degrees below zero, discovered by the physicists Penzias and Wilson in 1965. Everything besides that theory (such as superstrings, the wormhole, travel through time and parallel universes, plus some properties of black holes) is merely hypotheses with mathematical solutions that no observation has so far supported. Time will prove or disprove them, as the future of science and scientific discoveries is still at its beginning,

"And you have not been given of knowledge except a little."
