Russia's Sputnik writes the first letters of the space age – Hani Al-Dhulaie

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

It happened on 4 October 1957, when communist Russia outdid capitalist America and launched the first non-natural satellite in human history, Sputnik 1, which for the first time settled space superiority in favour of the Russians in their Cold War against the Americans, the two superpowers that dominated the world scene in the mid-twentieth century. Sputnik changed humanity's sense of its place in this universe.

Sputnik was a polished metal sphere no more than 70 centimetres across, fitted with four antennas. It kept beeping its radio waves from space for about three months until its batteries ran out, and then fell and burned in the Earth's atmosphere. Even radio amateurs who picked up its signals on 20 MHz heard its beeps, as it circled the Earth once every 92 minutes in a low orbit no higher than 400 kilometres.

Credit for sending Sputnik into space goes to the R-7 ballistic missile with a range of 3,700 kilometres, the first intercontinental missile developed in the Cold War, the same rocket that still works today sending satellites and astronauts. With this, the tiny satellite crushed the Americans' disdain for the Russians as a backward world of potato farmers.

Before the United States took any space action, Russia immediately, only a month later, launched a second satellite, Sputnik 2, carrying the dog Laika as the first animal to orbit the Earth.

These two feats astonished the Americans, who rethought their plans and launched their first satellite, Explorer, in January 1958, which is credited with discovering the so-called Van Allen radiation belts that wrap the Earth and protect it from the danger of cosmic rays and charged particles from the Sun.

Then came the creation of the US National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in October 1958 to keep up with the race to space, with a huge budget then estimated at 5% of the state's general budget.

But the Russians took the lead: the Russian spacecraft Luna 3 reached the Moon, circled it and photographed its far side in 1959, a side no human had ever seen, since the Moon always faces the Earth with one face. The Russians even won glory by sending Yuri Gagarin as the first astronaut in human history on 12 April 1961, which crushed American hopes in the space race and blocked their way for nearly ten years afterwards.

The far side of the Moon as seen in a NASA image taken in 2015; the Russians were the first to photograph this side, in 1959 (Reuters)

Then US President John Kennedy declared that before the end of the 1960s America must set the feet of its astronauts on the surface of the Moon, a dream fulfilled when Neil Armstrong reached the lunar surface in an unprecedented historic event in human life on 20 July 1969. The compass then began to swing towards American superiority in the space age.

The fevered space race between the two states continued until they reached Mars in the 1970s. Russia built its space fleet, represented by the Russian space station Mir, launched in 1986 and kept working until 2001, even after the launch of the International Space Station in 1998, which was an announcement of the end of the Cold War in space and the union of world powers to serve the Earth and humanity.

Sputnik's rise into orbit is, undisputedly, the opening of the space age. Sixty years later, space applications fill people's lives. The missions for which satellites are launched have diversified, and thousands now orbit the Earth, an estimated 15,000 satellites.

"Sputnik's rise into orbit is, undisputedly, the opening of the space age. Sixty years later, space applications fill people's lives."

Dozens of satellites are sent into orbit every year. There are remote-sensing satellites, which are credited with studying water resources, agricultural wealth, green areas, rates of desertification and quantities of groundwater. There are also weather satellites, which let the world learn the motion of winds, clouds, hurricanes, and amounts of rain and snow, and saved humanity from many dangers that threatened it from hurricanes sweeping the land with no warning.

There are spy and military satellites, the most expensive and most secret, able to photograph the Earth at a resolution of only a few centimetres from altitudes above 250 kilometres.

But the most important satellites that served humanity and turned the Earth into a small village are communications satellites, which secured television broadcasting, telephone calls, messages and modern social media. These satellites are geosynchronous and circle the Earth once every 24 hours; they are the ones to which we point the dishes on our rooftops, without ever having to change their direction, because they hold their position in the sky throughout their lives.

Satellites have a lifespan determined by their ability to stay in orbit at the same altitude and speed; otherwise their fate is to escape away or to fall and burn in the Earth's atmosphere, entering like a comet with a long tail whose fragments strike the Earth somewhere, which can be predicted in advance from the orbital calculations of the falling satellite.

The finest applications the space age has given us are the global positioning satellites (GPS), which can no longer be done without in most applications of daily life: they locate the person using a mobile phone, direct you on maps wherever you go on Earth, and guide air and sea navigation and fix the precise routes of their journeys.

Thanks to this enormous progress in the technologies of the space age, begun by the little Sputnik, mobile phone capabilities developed to include the digital camera, the internet, text, voice and video messages, and dozens or even hundreds of scientific and everyday applications gathered into an electronic device only a few inches in size.

Finally, how wonderful to tour the world without leaving your room: Google Earth lets you see the world with its maps, pictures, streets and landmarks if you have a computer and an internet connection. For a moment you may even see your home, your street and your car parked in front of your house, if you are lucky and the satellite passed over your city at that moment and recorded the scene to stay engraved in the memory of the giant computer until God wills.
