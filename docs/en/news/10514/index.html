Arabs to Mars

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

#مسبار_الأمل 🚀 Today is New Year's Day on Mars

By Eng. Majed Abu Zahra

Today, Sunday, 7 February 2021, Mars year 36 begins, marking a new year on the Red Planet.

The Earth and Mars formed together in the great primordial nebula in which the Sun also formed, and both are about 4.5 billion years old. As on Earth, a timekeeping system has been set up for Mars, compared with the most widely used calendar on Earth, the Gregorian calendar, which puts us in the year 2021.

The Martian calendar is much more recent: Mars year 1 began at the northern spring equinox on 11 April 1955, and a great dust storm swept Mars in the second half of that Martian year (sometimes called the great dust storm of 1956).

Martian years have no names, but the first system for numbering them was published in a scientific paper in 2000 describing temperature changes on the Red Planet.

The system was based on the orbit to count Martian years and to help compare data, and in the following years it came into use, especially among scientists who study the Martian climate.

The choice of 11 April 1955 was considered "arbitrary", but the great dust storm of 1956, which happened in Mars year 1, makes a convenient marker.

The system was later extended by setting Mars year 0 to begin on 24 May 1953, allowing negative year numbers, much like BC and AD in the Gregorian calendar (or, more recently, BCE and CE).

One year on Mars equals 687 Earth days, so Mars takes about twice as long as the Earth to orbit the Sun. This means a person's age would be much lower on Mars: simply divide your age by 1.88 to get your age on the planet.

As on Earth, a day on Mars is set by the time the planet takes to rotate on its axis. This is called a Martian solar day (sol), and it is slightly longer than an Earth day, at 24 hours and 39 minutes.

Mars has four seasons, winter, spring, summer and autumn, determined by the planet's position in its orbit around the Sun, and the new Martian year begins at the northern equinox (northern spring, southern autumn).

As Mars moves along its yearly path around the Sun, the planet's axial tilt makes its northern hemisphere receive more sunlight during northern summer and its southern hemisphere more sunlight during northern winter, just as on Earth. Unlike the Earth's seasons, however, the seasons on Mars are not equal in length.

This is because Mars's orbit around the Sun is more elliptical than the Earth's. For example, northern spring (southern autumn) is the longest season at 194 sols, and northern autumn (southern spring) is the shortest at 142 sols.

Mars's elliptical orbit can have important consequences: during southern spring and summer, Mars moves closer to the Sun and faster, and the extra sunlight warms the atmosphere, causing turbulence that lifts very fine particles from the Martian soil. That is why the second half of the Martian year is often marked by severe dust storms that can sometimes spread across the whole planet.

As on Earth, winters are cold and summers warm on Mars, but the planet as a whole is much colder, with an average annual temperature of 60 degrees below zero, and it experiences different weather phenomena through the seasons.

One phenomenon is seen every year in southern spring or summer: a cloud of ice crystals up to 1,800 kilometres long appears over the volcano Arsia Mons, recurring for at least 80 sols before disappearing for the rest of the year.
