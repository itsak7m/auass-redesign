The Sharjah Astronomical Observatory records a series of meteoroid impacts on the Moon

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

The Sharjah Astronomical Observatory continuously follows this type of event and shares its results with other observatories in various countries around the world

The Sharjah Astronomical Observatory at the Sharjah Academy for Astronomy, Space Sciences and Technology at the University of Sharjah recorded a sequential impact of meteoroid bodies on the Moon's surface on 18 January.

The Emirates News Agency said that Mohammed Fadl Talafha, a researcher on the observatory team, recorded this phenomenon, and that the Sharjah Astronomical Observatory continuously follows this type of event and shares its results with other observatories in various countries around the world, in addition to involving students in various research projects and observation missions.

Dr. Hamid Majul Al Naimi, Director of the University of Sharjah and Director General of the Sharjah Academy for Astronomy, Space Sciences and Technology, President of the Arab Union for Astronomy and Space Sciences, said the impacts were observed using the lunar meteoroid impact monitoring telescope of the Sharjah Astronomical Observatory, which consists of a three-metre-high dome equipped with a 14-inch telescope along with many sensitive instruments.

He explained that the lunar impact monitoring telescope watches the dark part of the Moon facing the Earth to determine the sizes of large meteoroids striking the Moon's surface and their rates of fall.

Dr. Mashhoor Ahmed Al Wardat, Deputy Director General of the Sharjah Academy for Astronomy, Space Sciences and Technology for Academic Affairs and the Sharjah Astronomical Observatory, revealed that by analysing the time of the impacts and their relative positions, the team concluded that they were a series of meteoroid impacts resulting from the break-up of the meteoroid body by the Moon's gravity as it approached its surface, as meteoroid bodies fragment and fall as pieces scattered lengthwise, as happened in several previous meteoroid strikes, the most famous being what happened to Comet Shoemaker–Levy when it approached the surface of Jupiter in 1994, when it fragmented and its pieces struck the planet in a long chain observed by astronomers from Earth at the time. It also happened to the asteroid that hit the Earth in 2008, which broke up and scattered its pieces over a long, wide area in the Nubian Desert in northern Sudan. Dr. Al Wardat explained that the Sharjah Astronomical Observatory team is now working on a deeper analysis of these lunar meteoroid strikes to determine the source of the original body and its mass.

Mohammed Fadl Talafha, the astronomical observer and research assistant at the observatory, explained that the monthly scheduled observation of meteoroid strikes on the Moon's surface with the lunar impact monitoring telescope dedicated to these observations requires high precision in analysis, as these impacts appear as very short flashes of light lasting no more than fractions of a second. He added that what distinguishes this series of impacts is that they occurred within one minute, were brighter than usual, and their flashes lasted a relatively long time, with durations of up to a quarter of a second recorded in each impact. The impacts were spread over the middle of the Moon's dark eastern side at the time of observation and extended over a distance of 1,000 km. Initial analyses of these impacts show that they created new lunar craters on the Moon's surface with diameters between 5 and 10 metres.

The Sharjah Astronomical Observatory specialises in recording meteoroid impacts on the Moon's surface with the aim of improving understanding of their effects on crewed space missions to the Moon, which helps ensure their safety. In addition, observing these phenomena makes it possible to study their types and their effect on the Moon's surface, and how lunar craters formed over billions of years.

It is noted that the Sharjah Astronomical Observatory recorded many impacts in the previous period, as the first observatory in the Arab countries dedicated to this type of observation, and recorded important cooperation with the European Space Agency in this regard.

The University of Sharjah has several graduate-level academic astronomy programmes, including the Master of Science in Space and Astronomy Sciences, the Master of Science in Geographic Information Systems and Remote Sensing, and the Master of Air and Space Law, which the university offers in cooperation with the Sharjah Academy for Astronomy, Space Sciences and Technology.
