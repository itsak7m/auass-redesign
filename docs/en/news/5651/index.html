Science news · 28 December 2019

How far did Arab space research get in 2019?

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

During 2019 the Arab world made ambitious achievements in the space sector and its research, in ambitious steps to keep pace with the global trend, which may bring the Arabs into the race for space travel in the future. Mostaqbal Observatory followed those Arab achievements, in the hope that they may be a glimmer of hope for future generations; here are the most prominent.

Saudi Arabia takes part in travel to the Moon

For the first time in human history, the People's Republic of China succeeded, at the start of 2019, in landing the uncrewed Chang'e 4 probe successfully on the far, unseen side of the Moon, in a long-awaited journey that humanity had put off for decades, with participation from the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The probe landed in the South Pole basin carrying equipment to conduct biological experiments and analyse the geology of the unexplored region of the Moon.

Saudi Arabia took part for the first time in its history in a space flight, after months of joint space research with the People's Republic of China. A team of Saudi researchers from the King Abdulaziz City for Science and Technology worked on designing and building space sensing systems to capture images of the Moon and provide scientific data to space science researchers. They measured less than 10.5 cubic centimetres and weighed no more than 630 grams, and were integrated into the Chinese satellite.

The first Iraqi woman working on developing NASA rockets

The young Diana Alsindy became the first Iraqi woman engineer working in the field of developing peaceful rockets for space exploration and building satellite engines at the US National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA). Alsindy currently works with Virgin Orbit as a rocket propulsion development engineer, on a rocket project called LauncherOne, which is distinguished by its low manufacturing cost and its ability to carry satellites from anywhere in the world. Most space rockets are launched vertically, whereas LauncherOne can be launched from beneath the left wing of a Boeing 747, without the need for fixed vertical launch pads. Diana Alsindy launched a page on Instagram, which she says aims to encourage others to enter the fields of science, engineering, mathematics and technology, particularly girls in the Arab world.

A Jordanian space scientist joins the Blue Moon project

In September the Jordanian space scientist Dr. Mohammad Taha Ababneh joined the Blue Moon Lander team, the most exciting project to build the first permanent human base on the surface of the Moon, one of the projects of Blue Origin, a company specialising in building space rockets, which aims to build a permanent human station on the lunar surface.

Ababneh had provided NASA with an unprecedented innovation, a cooling system for spacecraft operating on Mars and the Moon, which he built and developed with a scientific team that worked under his leadership for months and included experts and researchers from Advanced Cooling Technologies, NASA and Lockheed Martin. The cooling system works with a performance efficiency more than three times that of the systems currently in use, which is an unprecedented achievement that may bring a qualitative leap in the world of cooling the electronics of future spacecraft and small satellite systems.

Sudan launches the first satellite in its history

In November Sudan launched the first satellite in its history, SRSS-1, which performs remote sensing tasks. The satellite was launched with China's help aboard the Chinese Long March-4B rocket from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center in Shanxi province in northern China.

Egypt launches its first communications satellites

With the aim of bridging the digital divide, Egypt launched its first communications satellite, Tiba-1, on 22 November from the Guiana Space Centre near the city of Kourou in French Guiana. Egypt contracted a consortium of Airbus and Thales Alenia Space to develop the satellite, which weighs about 5,640 kilograms and was designed to stay in its orbit for up to about 15 years.

The first satellite built by Arab scientists

In March the United Arab Emirates named the first Arab satellite 813, the fruit of cooperation between a number of Arab countries, built entirely by Arab scientists, engineers and technicians, in honour of the year 813, which saw the peak of Arab-Islamic civilisation in the age of al-Ma'mun and the flourishing of the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, capital of the Abbasid caliphate. The project to build the Arab satellite comes within the charter founding the Arab Space Collaboration Group, signed on a UAE initiative in Abu Dhabi, to be the first space grouping of its kind bringing together under its umbrella 11 Arab countries.

The UAE intends to run space tourism flights

In the same month the UAE Space Agency signed a memorandum of understanding with Virgin Galactic and The Spaceship Company to study opportunities for running space tourism flights from UAE territory in the future. The memorandum also included defining frameworks of cooperation between the signatories in launching space flights for scientific, technical and educational purposes, as well as planning to build a SpaceShip spacecraft and a carrier aircraft to operate from the UAE, cooperating to establish a Centre of Excellence for Microgravity Research in the UAE, and setting operational plans for the spacecraft at Al Ain airport. The aim is for the UAE to become a scientific platform for space research and a station for running space tourism flights.

Launch of the UAE Space Strategy 2030

In the same month the UAE also launched its National Space Sector Strategy 2030, whose implementation is overseen by the UAE Space Agency to organise and develop the sector at the regional and global levels and strengthen its contribution to the local economy. The strategy aims to guide the concerned parties, companies working in and operating the space sector, and the various local projects towards ways of localising it and driving its progress, by carrying out ambitious space programmes and missions, strengthening research and development efforts, widening the scope of space exploitation and the opportunities for scientific and technical benefit from space applications, creating an effective regulatory environment, developing specialised expertise, attracting the top minds and stimulating creativity among young people, as well as building practical partnerships between industrial, educational and research institutions, and strengthening cooperation at the regional and local level, according to the Emirates Center for Strategic Studies and Research.

The UAE meteor-monitoring network

In late May the UAE launched the UAE Meteor Monitoring Network, the fruit of cooperation between the UAE Space Agency and the Sharjah Center for Astronomy and Space Sciences. The network consists of three different stations spread across the UAE to record astronomical phenomena in the country's sky. Each station includes astronomical cameras pointed at the sky, which record automatically as soon as meteors are detected, whether the meteor is part of a meteor shower or a piece of space debris. When a meteor is captured from more than one location, the network calculates its path to determine its source. Its three stations contain 51 specialised cameras that monitor the sky automatically. One camera in each station has a wide-field lens, which makes it easier to identify bright meteors that may produce a meteorite reaching the Earth, while the remaining cameras in each station have a narrow field of view, allowing meteors to be analysed more precisely and the meteor's orbit around the Sun to be plotted.

United Arab Emirates University gains membership of the international space research committee

In mid-May the National Space Science and Technology Center at United Arab Emirates University gained membership of the Committee on Space Research (COSPAR).

An Emirati Mars rover

Emirati university students developed a Mars rover that can drive and work on the Red Planet to help astronauts get around during future missions. The ambitious project was carried out by Anoud Al Kathiri, Fatima Al Naimi, Mai Al Mazrouei, Safiya Mohammed and Shaikha Al Naqbi, students in the Department of Mechanical Engineering in the College of Engineering at UAE University, under the supervision of Professor Dr. Mohammed Younes. The team named the new vehicle the RAM Mars rover, and took into account in building and developing it the particular conditions of the Martian surface and climate. The RAM Mars rover weighs about 450 kilograms and runs on four batteries each weighing 36 kilograms, rechargeable with solar energy, and the batteries in turn supply electric motors that turn the car's wheels.

Simulating life on Mars

In July the University of Dubai announced its intention to launch an ambitious project to simulate life on Mars in a special laboratory, in cooperation with the Chinese group World Mission World, at the Mission Z space laboratory, which gives UAE students a chance to experience life in space and work alongside experts and researchers specialised in space and future technologies.

Space science research programme for undergraduates

At the start of November the UAE launched the 2020 Space Science Research Programme for undergraduate students, as an ambitious step towards achieving the UAE Space Strategy. The Mohammed bin Rashid Space Centre opened registration for the sixth round of the space science research programme for non-graduating Emirati undergraduates next summer, in the fields of science and engineering. UAE University had previously offered a space science track for College of Science students, as one of the specialised tracks in the Physics department, offering topics related to satellite launch technology, planets and their exploration, the atmosphere of the Earth and planets, and the use of remote sensing in space science studies, as an academic track that mirrors global programmes to build a generation of specialists in space science in the future.

The Hope Probe

In the first Arab project to explore the Red Planet, the UAE intends to launch the Hope Probe from Earth in July 2020, to reach Mars at the start of 2021, coinciding with the 50th anniversary of the founding of the state. The project provides new additions to international scientific studies of the Red Planet and ambitious expectations for understanding climate change on Earth. The probe consists of a compact hexagonal spacecraft, its design resembling honeycomb cells, made of aluminium with a rigid structure and light weight, protected by a reinforced cover of composite sheets. Its overall size and weight are similar to a small car, weighing about 1,500 kilograms including the weight of the fuel, with a width of 2.37 metres and a length of 2.90 metres.

The first Arab astronaut enters the International Space Station

In September the UAE crowned its efforts in the space field with the arrival of the first Emirati and Arab astronaut at the International Space Station, as Emirati astronaut Hazza Al Mansouri entered the station after a journey of about 6 hours aboard the Soyuz MS-15 spacecraft, which launched from the Russian Baikonur cosmodrome in Kazakhstan. Al Mansouri is 35 years old. He holds a bachelor's degree in aviation sciences, majoring as a military pilot, from the Khalifa bin Zayed Air College. He has fourteen years of experience in military aviation and underwent a set of training programmes in the UAE and abroad, including advanced specialised courses in drowning survival, centrifuge training with acceleration forces of up to 9 g, and Red Flag exercises in the United States. In 2016 he qualified as a solo aerobatic display pilot, and currently serves as an FCF pilot on the F-16 Block 60. During the flight Al Mansouri took part with the team of other astronauts in scientific research in space, 16 experiments, including 6 experiments aboard the station.
