Saturday: Apophis will approach our planet

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

On Saturday 6 March 2021 the Earth sees the asteroid Apophis, about 370 metres in diameter, pass at a distance of 16,455,766 kilometres, roughly 40 times the distance between the Earth and the Moon, at 04:15 am Saudi time (01:15 GMT). It will not collide with our planet, but will serve as preparation for the big approach in 2029.

Asteroid Apophis will be well placed for observation, which will give a chance to study it, especially using radar, where a powerful beam will be sent to the asteroid and a radio telescope then used to catch the echo that bounces back. That complex data will be processed, and the result is an image resembling sound waves, which will provide detailed information on this asteroid with the aim of improving predictions of what may happen 8 years from now.

In general, asteroid Apophis will be very faint when it approaches on Saturday, so it cannot be seen through small telescopes, but it is within reach of telescopes 12 inches across and larger, and perhaps even smaller telescopes using sensitive cameras.

These observations of asteroid Apophis will improve our knowledge of its shape and its rotation on its axis, and this will help reduce the uncertainty in the asteroid's orbit caused by the Yarkovsky effect.

Apophis has been closely monitored since its discovery in 2004, and it has become known that an impact with Earth is ruled out in 2029 and 2036, but recent results indicate there is a very small chance of an impact in April 2068, although earlier calculations in 2016 ruled out the possibility of an impact in 2068; new results will appear soon.

Apophis, like many other asteroids, has been classified as a potentially hazardous asteroid, and according to some estimates an asteroid the size of Apophis is likely to strike the Earth about once every 80,000 years.

In recent times two asteroids have struck the Earth, one destroying the Siberian Tunguska forests in 1908, while the other broke up in the sky over Chelyabinsk, Russia, in 2013. But they are nothing compared with Apophis, which is 300 times bigger than the Tunguska asteroid and 5,000 times bigger than the Chelyabinsk asteroid, so this is an object that draws attention.

Now, scientists will use Apophis's approach to test their ability to deal with potentially hazardous asteroids, which will improve planetary defence, a programme focused on identifying space rocks that may pose a danger to Earth, mapping their precise paths and seeing how their orbits compare with the Earth's orbit.

If computer models show that the asteroid's and the Earth's orbits will reach the same place at the same time, things become dangerous, especially when the space rock is large. This is the kind of scenario that ended the age of the dinosaurs. But if a dangerous asteroid such as Apophis or others is identified sufficiently long before impact, then in theory something can be done to divert its path.

Success in preventing damage from an asteroid impact will depend on detecting the threat in time, which requires practice and experience. But although scientists have identified more than 25,000 near-Earth asteroids so far, the majority are too small to cause much concern, so while there are many asteroids orbiting around the Earth's orbit, most are not large enough or close enough to cause realistic existential worry.

Making the most of Apophis's 2029 approach will rest on the important baseline data that will be collected this year, and it is an excellent opportunity to test international capabilities to detect and track near-Earth objects and assess the collective response to any real threat from any asteroid in the future.
