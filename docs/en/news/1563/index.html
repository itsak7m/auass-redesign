The historic meeting of Earth and Mars in 2018 – Hani Al-Dhulaie

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

After fifteen years Mars returns to shine brightly in Earth's sky. It is the historic meeting that recurs only once every 15 years, a meeting that invites all astronomers, amateurs and sky-lovers to pay attention to this light-footed visitor. In 2003, on the occasion of that historic visit and the very close approach of the two planets to only 55.7 million kilometres, two space vehicles were sent to Mars to land in January 2004, Spirit and Opportunity, after a journey of six and a half months; the first carried out its mission for six years and then fell silent, while the second kept sending us data and images to this day, and is credited with the latest discovery of organic material announced by NASA a few weeks ago. Why Mars? Mars is one of the terrestrial planets and the farthest of them from the Sun; it has a solid ground like Mercury, Venus and Earth, and is relatively small, with a diameter of 6,800 kilometres, about half that of the Earth. Its year is 687 Earth days, nearly two Earth years. Its day is about the Earth's, 24.5 hours.

Seasons of Mars

What most distinguishes Mars, however, is that the axis of its rotation around the Sun is tilted at an angle of 24 degrees, close to the tilt of the Earth's axis (23.5 degrees), which means that it goes through seasons like those we have on Earth with one difference: its seasons are twice as long as Earth's, about six months each. In terms of habitability, Mars is the nearest planet to Earth because of its relatively moderate surface temperature compared with the other planets, reaching a high of minus 20, and because of the thin atmosphere around it, made up of gases in small proportions such as carbon dioxide and nitrogen. The density of this atmosphere is a hundred times lower than on Earth's surface. Even so, this thin atmosphere is able to blanket the whole planet in a sandstorm called a Martian storm, after which the surface features of Mars disappear from the lenses of telescopes and observatories that can make out some dark features on its surface, and even the white southern polar ice cap that appears clearly when winter arrives there. Even small telescopes can do so, especially in its greatest season, the historic meeting with Earth once every fifteen years. The red colour that distinguishes Mars is due to a large amount of iron oxide (rust) on its surface, which indicates that water was abundant there, in addition to the dust storms that often sweep its surface.

The cycle of Mars

Since Mars orbits outside Earth's orbit in a cycle of less than two Earth years, it meets the Earth at a close point about once every two years (2.13 years). This cycle is called the synodic cycle because it lines up Mars, Earth and the Sun in one line at the least distance between them, and the alignment of the three bodies is called opposition, with the Earth in the middle between the Sun and Mars: as the Sun sets in the west, Mars rises on the eastern horizon. The day of this opposition is 27/7/2018, the great opposition of the two planets; they approach every two years, but the distance between them this time falls to only 57 million kilometres, close to the distance at the previous great opposition on 27/8/2003, that is, fifteen years earlier. Its greatest distance from Earth is 400 million kilometres when it is on the far side of its orbit around the Sun. But in its conjunction every two years it approaches to about 70 million kilometres, a distance relatively far from the reach of amateur telescopes and cameras, especially as its diameter is only half the Earth's.

What we will see on Mars's surface

As the planet approaches Earth little by little, and with winter at its south pole, the white ice cap has begun to appear in the lenses of relatively large amateur telescopes, though the expected meeting will let small telescopes see it too, even those three inches (7.5 cm) across. Because the Martian atmosphere is thin, and if it is calm and not raising dust, the surface features of Mars will also be plain and clear. The most important of these features is a huge feature named Arabia for its resemblance to it, a light-coloured region adjacent to a dark feature called in Latin Syrtis Major, the Libyan Gulf lying on the coast of Sidra in Libya on Earth. So the name Libya exists as one of the features marked on the map of Mars, as do Syria, Aden, Moab and Sinai. If we examine the map carefully we will find Arab cities and villages, chosen at random, whose names were given by the International Astronomical Union to Martian impact craters. Finally, Mars always shines among the stars with its deep red colour, moving among them at a noticeable speed from one day to the next, and it is the best of the planets for showing us the phenomenon of retrograde motion, because it has an orbit outside Earth's: as it moves from west to east it suddenly stops and begins to move from east to west, the opposite of its first motion, then after a few days stops again and resumes its first motion as before. All the outer planets beyond it share this phenomenon, but it is less visible and noticeable. On this astronomical occasion, activities to observe the Red Planet are held in most Arab countries from Friday 27 July to the end of the month in various Arab cities, using telescopes that will let people see some features of this planet, which is expected to become a destination for visitors from Earth and its inhabitants within the next two decades.
