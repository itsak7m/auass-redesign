Lectures and events · 30 January 2019

Lecture by Prof. Osama Shalabiea: "Radio astronomy, an eye on the invisible universe"

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

At the invitation of Dr. Eng. Awni Khasawneh, Director of the Regional Centre for Space Science and Technology Education for Western Asia, Prof. Osama Shalabiea, professor of astrophysics and space physics and head of the Department of Astronomy, Space Science and Meteorology at the Faculty of Science, Cairo University, gave a lecture entitled "Radio astronomy: an eye on the invisible universe", as part of the centenary of the International Astronomical Union under the theme "100 years under one sky". It was attended by many researchers and people interested in astronomy and space technology. The lecturer began by reviewing the main stages in the evolution of the universe since its beginning in the Big Bang, and how its components took the form we see today. Because they are so far away, scientists and engineers devised methods to study the properties of stars and galaxies based on spectral analysis of the electromagnetic waves they emit, and many advanced telescopes have been sent into space to study and analyse these waves. Some telescopes specialise in X-ray spectra and others in gamma rays (since these rays cannot pass through the Earth's atmosphere).
Radio waves, however, can pass through, which has allowed scientists to design ground-based telescopes for this purpose, radio telescopes, spread widely across the globe. Radio astronomy is one of the most important branches of astronomy and astrophysics for studying many phenomena across the vast universe. The lecturer reviewed the place radio astronomy holds: radio observations let us learn the structure of our galaxy, the Milky Way, and of other galaxies millions of light years away, and study thermal and non-thermal phenomena and their distribution in the universe. Radio astronomy also helps scientists study the strength and distribution of magnetic fields between stars and galaxies. And thanks to radio astronomy, he added, scientists have been able to study the dust grains floating in space and learn about the primordial matter from which stars and planets formed.
The lecturer reviewed the main radio astronomy research centres around the world, such as NRAO, ATNF and ASTRON. He also praised the role of the Director General of the Royal Jordanian Geographic Centre in establishing a radio observatory in Jordan as a starting point towards a specialised research centre that keeps pace with international centres in this field.
The lecture, which lasted an hour and a half, was followed by many comments and questions. The lecturer praised the quality of the discussion and called on the rising generation of young researchers to keep up with the rapid theoretical and technical progress in radio astronomy. He expressed the hope for a joint research centre for Arab scientists, supported by Arab governments, to build radio observatories across the Arab world, in the hope of one day having an Arab Radio Telescope (ART).
