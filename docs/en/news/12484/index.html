The Webb Space Telescope and the journey back in time

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

The Webb Space Telescope and the journey back in time

The power of the Webb Space Telescope

The James Webb Space Telescope (Webb) is extremely sensitive and has high angular resolution, able to detect very faint light signals. Although quasars, or "quasi-stellar objects", are extremely bright, they are the most distant objects in the universe, so the signals Webb receives from them will be very, very faint. Webb is able to separate the light of a quasar from the remaining light of its galaxy.

Webb will be the world's premier space science observatory when it is launched this year. Scientists hope Webb will help them solve many of the mysteries of our solar system that remain somewhat unclear, and look farther, to galaxies and other very distant objects we may never have known before! They hope Webb will offer answers about mysterious structures, the origins of the universe and our place in it.

It is an international programme led by NASA with its partners, the European Space Agency and the Canadian Space Agency.

Quasars to reveal the secrets of the early universe:

Webb will be able to look into the past, observing quasars as they appeared billions of years ago!

Quasars outshine all the stars of their galaxy combined and are among the brightest objects in the universe. They are active, extremely bright supermassive black holes with masses millions to billions of times that of the Sun. They usually lie at the centres of galaxies, feed on infalling matter and pour out radiation in a beautiful way, and they can shape the galaxies in which they reside.

Scientists will use Webb to study six of the brightest and most distant quasars together with their host galaxies, to learn how they are connected and what role quasars play in the evolution of galaxies during the earliest stages of the universe. Webb will also help scientists study the gas between galaxies, especially during the period of cosmic reionization. The extreme sensitivity and angular resolution of Webb's instruments will make these studies possible.

Visiting the young universe

Because Webb looks deep into the universe, it is in effect looking back in time. The light from these quasars began its journey to Webb when the universe was very young and took billions of years to arrive, giving Webb the chance to see the universe's components as they were long ago, while they were forming.

Santiago Arribas, professor of astrophysics at the Centre for Astrobiology in Madrid, Spain, and a member of the NIRSpec team, explained: "All these quasars we are studying existed very early, when the universe was less than a billion years old, less than 6% of its current age. These observations and measurements give us the opportunity to study the evolution of galaxies and the formation of supermassive black holes at those times."

Light from these very distant objects is shifted towards the red by the expansion of the universe, as general relativity predicts; this is known as cosmological redshift. The farther the light source (the quasar) is from the observer (Webb), the greater its redshift. In fact, visible light emitted in the early universe is shifted so much that by the time it reaches us it has moved into infrared (IR) wavelengths, and Webb has been equipped with highly sensitive instruments that make it uniquely suited to this task.

Studying quasars, their host galaxies and environments, and their powerful outflows

The quasars under study are not only among the most distant in the universe but also among the brightest. They usually contain black holes of high mass with high accretion rates, the rates at which matter falls into black holes, the most fearsome objects in the universe because of their intense gravity.

Chris Willott, a researcher at the National Research Council of Canada's Herzberg Astronomy and Astrophysics Research Centre and Webb project scientist for the Canadian Space Agency, said: "We're interested in observing the most luminous quasars because the very high amount of energy that they're generating has the greatest impact on their galaxy through outflows and heating. We want to observe these quasars at the moment when they're having the biggest impact on their host galaxies."

An enormous amount of energy is released when matter is crushed by the supermassive black hole and pushes the surrounding gas outwards, creating powerful outflows that sweep through the stars like a tsunami and wreak havoc in the galaxy.

These outflows play an important role in galaxy evolution: the rate of star formation falls when the gas is expelled, and in some cases star formation stops completely when large amounts of gas are driven out.

Scientists believe these outflows are the main mechanism by which gas, dust and elements are redistributed over large distances within a galaxy or even expelled into the space between galaxies, the intergalactic medium, helping to bring about fundamental changes in the properties of the galaxy and of the intergalactic medium.

Examining the properties of intergalactic space during the era of reionization

More than 13 billion years ago the universe was opaque to light, and so it was dark. Over hundreds of millions of years the gas between galaxies became charged, or ionised, making it transparent to ultraviolet (UV) light. This period is called the Era of Reionization, one of the main frontiers of astrophysics, which scientists hope to study in detail with the help of Webb's data and observations of this transition.

To study the universe we need very bright light sources, and the quasar is the ideal source in the distant universe because we can see it well. The gas absorbs specific wavelengths of the quasar's light, and imaging spectroscopy is used to study and analyse these absorption lines in the gas; the brighter the quasar, the stronger the absorption features in its spectrum. By determining whether the gas is neutral or ionised, scientists will learn how neutral the universe was and how far reionization had progressed at that particular time.

Team member Camilla Pacifici of the Space Telescope Science Institute said: "We want to study the early universe because it is evolving, and we want to know how it started."

The light from the quasars will be analysed with NIRSpec to search for "metals", elements heavier than hydrogen and helium. These elements formed in the first stars and galaxies and were expelled by outflows, so the gas moves out of the galaxies where it originally was and into the intergalactic medium. The team plans to measure the production of these first "metals" and the way they were pushed into the intergalactic medium by these early outflows.
