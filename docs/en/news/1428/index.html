How are comets born?

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Do comets result from collisions of relatively large bodies, or do they date from the early formation of the solar system? Analysis of data from the Rosetta spacecraft shows the origin of comets and how they formed. Read this article to learn more: #Rosetta #Comets #SolarSystem.

Detailed analysis of the data collected by the Rosetta spacecraft shows that comets are remnants of the early formation of the solar system, not more recent fragments produced by later collisions between other, larger bodies. Understanding how and when comets such as 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko took their shapes is of the utmost importance in determining how they can be used to explain the early formation and evolution of the solar system. In this regard, a new study centred on this question was published in Astronomy & Astrophysics, led by Björn Davidsson of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory at the California Institute of Technology in Pasadena (USA). If comets are primitive bodies, they can help reveal the properties of the solar nebula from which the Sun, planets and small bodies formed 4.6 billion years ago, as well as the processes that turned our planetary system into what we see. The alternative hypothesis holds that comets are more recent fragments produced by collisions between older bodies such as Neptune's icy trans-Neptunian objects (TNOs). They would then shed light on the internal structure of such larger bodies, the collisions that shattered them, and the process by which new bodies form from the remains of older ones. Matt Taylor, ESA's Rosetta project scientist, says: "Either way, comets are witnesses to important events in the evolution of the solar system, which is why we made these detailed measurements with Rosetta, along with explorations of other comets, in order to find the most likely scenario." During its two years at comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko, Rosetta returned a picture of the comet as a low-density, highly porous, bilobed body with extended layers, indicating that the lobes are material accumulated over time before merging. The unusually high porosity of the interior gives the first sign that this growth could not have happened through violent collisions, as that would compress the fragile materials. The structures and features seen by Rosetta's cameras across different size ranges give more information about how this evolution happened.

Earlier studies, on the other hand, showed that the head and body were originally two separate bodies, but the collisions that merged them must have been of low speed so as not to destroy them. The fact that roughly the same layers exist in both bodies indicates that they must have gone through the same evolutionary history, and also means that survival rates against catastrophic collisions must have been high for a long time. Merger events may also have happened on smaller scales. For example, three spherical peaks have been identified in the Bastet region on the small lobe of the comet, and the explanation is that they are the results of smaller comets still partly preserved today. At smaller scales, only a few metres across, there are so-called "wrinkles" (undulations) and "hills", and in addition rugged features have been observed in many pits and exposed cliff walls at different places on the comet. While this formation could have arisen from fragmentation alone, another view holds that they actually represent internal clumps of the comet's components; these folds could show the typical size of the smaller comets that accumulated and merged to form the comet, visible today because of the erosion caused by sunlight. According to the theory, the speeds at which smaller comets collide and merge change during the growth process, giving rise to peaks when the clumps reach several metres in size. For this reason features several metres across are thought to be the most compact and resilient, which is particularly interesting since the comet's material appears nodular and clumped especially at this size range. Other lines of evidence include spectral analysis of the comet's composition, which shows that the surface saw little or no alteration by liquid water, and analysis of gases released from ice layers rising from deep beneath the surface, which showed the comet is rich in highly volatile particles such as carbon monoxide, oxygen, nitrogen and argon. These observations indicate that comets formed in extremely cold conditions and saw no major thermal processing for most of their lives. Instead they must have accumulated slowly over a long period, which explains the low temperatures, the survival of some ices and the retention of highly volatile particles. Björn says: "While the larger TNOs in the outer parts of the solar system seem to have been heated by short-lived radioactive material, comets show no similar signs of such heating. We must resolve this contradiction by looking at the timeline of models of our solar system and taking new ideas into account."

Björn and colleagues assumed that the larger bodies in the TNO population formed quickly during the first million years of the solar nebula's life, helped by turbulent gas streams that accelerated their growth to sizes of up to 400 km. For about three million years of the solar system's history, the gas vanished from the solar nebula, leaving only solids. Later, over a longer period of about 400 million years, TNOs slowly accumulated more material and were compressed into layers, and their ices melted and refroze, so that some TNOs grew to roughly the size of Pluto or Triton. Comets, by contrast, took a different path: after the initial rapid growth phase of the TNOs, grainy surfaces (with bumps) and "pebbles" of icy material in the cold outer part of the solar nebula began gathering at low speed, giving comets about 5 km across, while the gas was vanishing from the solar nebula. The low speeds at which the material accumulated produced bodies with fragile, highly porous nuclei of low density. This slow growth let comets keep some of the ancient, highly volatile material of the solar nebula, as they were able to release the energy generated by radioactive decay inside them without heating up much. The larger TNOs played a greater role in the evolution of comets, influencing their orbits, which led to additional material merging at somewhat higher speed over the following 25 million years and forming the comets' outer layers. This influence let these bodies, several kilometres across, collide gently with each other, giving some comets a bilobed nature. Björn concludes: "Comets do not appear to show the properties expected of collisional rubble piles, resulting from the crushing of larger bodies such as TNOs. Instead we think they grew in the shadow of TNOs, essentially surviving unharmed for 4.6 billion years." Matt adds: "Our new model explains what we see in Rosetta's detailed observations of its comet, and what was hinted at in earlier missions that flew by comets, the real buried treasures of the solar system." He ends by saying: "Comets give us an unprecedented idea about the processes that mattered in planet formation in those early times, and how that relates to the structure of the solar system we see today."
