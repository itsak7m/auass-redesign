Asteroid 2010 GD37 hitting Earth is just a rumour

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Prepared by Eng. Majed Abu Zahra

Some websites and social media spread incorrect information attributed to NASA indicating that asteroid (2010 GD37), with a diameter of 1.26 kilometres, will hit the Earth next December.

It must be stressed that NASA has issued no warning, and asteroid (2010 GD37) is rated at level (zero) on the Torino scale for the impact hazard of asteroids and comets. For an asteroid to pose a danger it must be at level (five) to be a cause for concern for the Earth.

The best orbital data indicate that the asteroid will be outside the orbit of Mars or slightly inside it in December, and will even be on the opposite side of the Sun from the Earth on that date. Had we assumed it would hit the Earth, all the telescopes around the world would be tracking the asteroid now, especially the new early-warning system (ATLAS), which gives warning time a year before an asteroid a kilometre across or larger hits, with accuracy approaching 100%. So there is no danger of this asteroid hitting the Earth, and the asteroid's orbit is likely to remain stable for millions of years into the future.

It should be known that each year many asteroids are put on the "potential hazard" list, and currently there are 39 asteroids in 2019, the smallest being asteroid (2008 EK68), four metres across, and the largest (2010 GD37), 1.26 kilometres across.

This may sound frightening but in reality it is different: the asteroids are at level (zero), which means that their chance of impact is so low as to be effectively zero.

Asteroids put on the "potential hazard" list are regularly removed from that list after further observations. More than 2,000 asteroids have already been removed, so the most likely scenario is that all 39 asteroids will be removed from that list too.

From time to time a very small asteroid can be tracked and its impact predicted, and after entering the Earth's atmosphere it will be merely a bright fireball crossing the sky. This has happened several times before, though some come unannounced, as happened in the skies over Russia in 2013.

It is known that 95% of asteroids one kilometre or more across have been detected, and most of them pose no threat to the Earth. There is an asteroid of that size called (1950 DA) with a 0.012% chance of hitting the Earth in the year 2880, more than eight and a half centuries in the future.

It is very unlikely that asteroids among the remaining 5% of that size have any chance of hitting our planet in this century, or even for several centuries, once their orbits are well known.

We already know all the asteroids 10 kilometres across or larger, and none of them will hit the Earth for thousands of years.

If we were to deal with the worst case, that means that one of the asteroids in the remaining 5% is on a collision course with the Earth and hits us a year after its discovery, or perhaps there is a comet one kilometre across. But that is unlikely, as short-period comets of this size are included in the 95%, and comets with longer orbital periods are very rare, and these pose no danger according to current data.

It should be known that the dome of the sky is under constant, round-the-clock observation by many observatories around the world, searching for large asteroids that may not be known and that could harm our planet, and determining their paths through space in the near future.

The near-Earth object monitoring programme known as "Sentry" detects these objects, determines their physical nature and predicts their paths to determine whether they could pose a danger to our planet.

To date there are no real threats, except that meteorites fall constantly in the form of annual or random meteor showers, which are harmless, along with small asteroids burning up in the atmosphere.

Moreover, we have not been hit by an asteroid a kilometre across since the days of Neanderthal man 400,000 years ago.

So the story of asteroid (2010 GD37) hitting the Earth in December is an extension of earlier rumours about celestial bodies, especially asteroids, giving them an exaggeratedly frightening colouring.
