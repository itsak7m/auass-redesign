Earth's mini-moon 2020 SO makes a close approach on Tuesday

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

By Eng. Majed Abu Zahra

On Tuesday, 2 February 2021, the 10-metre object 2020 SO, which became a mini-moon of the Earth a few months ago, will make a close approach at a distance of 220,000 kilometres. There is no risk of it hitting the Earth, and it will not be visible to the naked eye.

The object was first observed in September 2020 with the Pan-STARRS telescope at Haleakala, Hawaii, and computer models showed that the Earth would capture it temporarily as a new mini-moon; it has indeed been orbiting the Earth since 8 November 2020.

After its motion was analysed for three months, and after it passed within 50,000 kilometres of our planet on 1 December 2020, it was confirmed that the object is not an asteroid at all but a Centaur booster from the 1960s, specifically from the Surveyor 2 lunar mission, launched more than 50 years ago.

Measurements indicate that the object was pushed off its original path by the small but continuous pressure of sunlight, which affects a hollow object more than a solid one. A spent rocket stage is essentially an empty tube, a low-density object with a large surface area, so it is pushed by solar radiation pressure more than a dense solid rock, just as an empty soda can is blown by the wind more than a small stone.

This is not the first time the Earth has captured a mini-moon. Space is full of small asteroids, and from time to time one of these space rocks is temporarily captured by our planet's gravity before being flung back out into the solar system.

There are two confirmed mini-moons: 2006 RH120 (in Earth orbit between 2006 and 2007) and 2020 CD3 (in our orbit between 2018 and 2020).

This approach of 2020 SO will be its second and last: the Earth's gravity will release it in March 2021 and it will no longer be a mini-moon of the Earth, but will instead orbit the Sun.
