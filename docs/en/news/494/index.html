Science news · 20 January 2017

Al-Hasan Ibn al-Haytham in brief – Dr. Abdulmajeed Nusair

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Dr. Abdulmajeed Nusair

Professor Emeritus, Jordan University of Science and Technology

Member of the Academy of the Arabic Language in Jordan

President of the Jordanian Society for the History of Science

Abu Ali al-Hasan ibn al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham was born in Basra in 354 AH / 965 AD, of Arab origin. He was small in body, short and slight, but he loved science and knowledge, was a keen observer and kept away from idle amusement. Ibn Abi Usaybi'a said of him: "Ibn al-Haytham was noble of character, strong in intellect, skilled in many sciences. None of his contemporaries matched him. He was always at work, wrote a great deal, was very ascetic, loved doing good, had a fine hand and a good command of Arabic." (Uyun al-Anba', p. 550).

He travelled in search of knowledge to many regions, to Baghdad, the Levant and Egypt. Al-Bayhaqi said: "He stayed in the Levant with one of its emirs, who provided for him and gave him a great deal of money. Abu Ali said, 'My daily bread is enough for me,' and accepted only the expenses he needed and modest clothing." (Tarikh Hukama' al-Islam, p. 86).

He studied the many sciences translated from the Greeks and others, made summaries of some, and wrote and innovated on different subjects. He studied medicine in Baghdad and passed the practice examination first set by the Abbasid caliph al-Muqtadir in 921 AD. He also had considerable skill in mathematics, physics and scientific method. When he quoted others he was not ashamed to credit them. Al-Bayhaqi quotes him: "If you find words by someone else, do not attribute them to yourself; be content with having benefited from them, for a child belongs to his father and words to their author." (p. 88).

It was reported to the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah that Ibn al-Haytham had said in one of his gatherings in Basra: "If I were in Egypt I would do something with its Nile that would make it beneficial in every state, for I have heard that it descends from a high place at the edge of the Egyptian region." (Ibn al-Qifti, Ikhbar al-'Ulama', p. 114). The caliph summoned him and received him at one of the gates of Cairo with a grand welcome, gave him suitable lodging and a generous income. After a while he asked him to look into the matter of the Nile, equipped a scientific mission for him and sent him by boat along the Nile. He reached Aswan and then the region of the cataracts. Ibn al-Haytham examined the area and realised that what was required was too great for the craft of that age. He returned to Cairo and apologised to al-Hakim, who accepted his excuse. Then the caliph gave him an administrative post, which he accepted reluctantly; to be safe from al-Hakim's changeable moods he feigned madness. Al-Hakim gave him a house, an allowance and someone to look after him. After al-Hakim's death Ibn al-Haytham returned to his scientific work and no longer needed the sultan's stipend. He would copy three books in his fine hand (Euclid's Elements, the Mutawassitat of the Banu Musa ibn Shakir, and Ptolemy's Almagest), and someone would come and buy these copies for a hundred and fifty dinars, enough for his needs. (al-Qifti, p. 115). He was very productive in many fields of knowledge, and the total of his treatises and books came to 237.

He fell gravely ill at the end of his life. When he felt death approaching, he turned towards the qibla and said: "Geometry is lost, treatment and the medical sciences are void, and nothing remains but to surrender the soul to its Creator and Maker." (al-Qifti, p. 115). Finally he said: "To You is the return and to You the destination, my Lord; in You I trust and to You I turn." (al-Qifti, p. 115). Then his soul departed, may God have mercy on him, in 440 AH / 1039 AD.

Ibn al-Haytham lived in the golden age of science in Arab and Islamic civilisation, and his contemporaries included a large group of great scholars, among them al-Biruni, Ibn 'Iraq, Ibn Yunus, Ibn al-Samh, al-Khujandi, al-Karaji, Kushyar al-Jili, Ibn Sina, al-Nasawi, al-Kirmani and al-Saraqusti.

His main scientific achievements:

1- Scientific method: Ibn al-Haytham is regarded as a principal founder of the scientific method, centuries before the Europeans, although he followed in the footsteps of Jabir ibn Hayyan, who valued experiment and called it "dirba". Dr. Omar Farrukh summarised Ibn al-Haytham's method in the following points:

a- Induction: deriving the general rule from individual cases.

b- Deduction: deriving individual cases from the general rule.

c- Analogy: weighing different cases against each other and comparing results.

d- Observation: looking at things occurring in their particular environment.

e- Noting: spotting what agrees and what differs among these things.

f- Repeating observation and experiment to verify what a hypothesis says. (History of Arab Sciences)

K. Kohl saw Ibn al-Haytham's study of moonlight, in his "Treatise on the Light of the Moon", as "the first attempt at a detailed astrophysical study", in which he came "even close to studying moonlight with experimental tools". He was the first to use the camera obscura, which shows he was a natural investigator and "the founder of modern natural research". K. Kohl, Über das Licht des Mondes in SPPMSE 1926-1927/306.

2- Mathematics. Sezgin lists 36 works of his in mathematics whose manuscripts survive, a number edited by Roshdi Rashed, Ali Abdul-Latif and others, on plane and solid geometry, the properties of conic sections, arithmetic, number theory, isoperimetric problems (the surfaces or solids that are largest for equal perimeter, where the circle for surfaces and the sphere for solids meet the requirement), and more. We mention some in a little detail:

a- Ibn al-Haytham's problem: Europeans knew this problem and gave it special attention, so that it became known to them as Alhazen's Problem. Its mathematical statement is: given two points in the plane of a given circle, draw two lines from them meeting on the circumference so that they make equal angles with the normal to the circle at that point. In optics it reads: given two points A and B outside a surface, which need not be flat, find the point C on that surface such that AC is the incident ray and BC the reflected ray. Its general solution leads to a fourth-degree equation, which led him to sum series of fourth powers, generally to the power k, in the form:

(n+1) nΣi=1 ai = nΣi=1 ai+1 + nΣs=1 ( sΣi=1 ai )

He proved it for n = 1, 2, 3, 4, by a method close to mathematical induction, meaning it can be generalised to any positive integer n, and the answer is:

nΣn+1 a4 = (1/5 + 1/(5n)) n (n+1/2) [n(n+1) – 1/3)]

His calculation equals that of the following definite integral:

l∫0 x4 dx

Thus Ibn al-Haytham and al-Karaji were the pioneers of the method of mathematical induction. He thereby arrived at the principles of integration and used it to calculate the volume of a paraboloid. His problem occupied great European mathematicians until the eighteenth century.

b- In geometry he wrote "A book on resolving the doubts in Euclid's Elements and explaining its meanings" and "Commentary on Euclid's postulates". He tried to prove the fifth postulate (the parallel postulate), which led him to present a quadrilateral with three right angles, leaving the fourth angle's position open. If it is right, we get plane Euclidean geometry; if it is acute, the resulting geometry is hyperbolic non-Euclidean geometry. This quadrilateral is called today the Lambert quadrilateral, though it is more fitting to call it the Ibn al-Haytham–Lambert quadrilateral.

In number theory he made important contributions. He gave the first rule for perfect numbers (numbers equal to the sum of their factors, such as 6 = 1+2+3), namely 2n-1 (2n – 1) where 2n – 1 is prime. He could not prove it; Euler proved it in the eighteenth century. His work on congruences also led him to Wilson's theorem and the Chinese remainder theorem.

Sezgin also lists 35 titles in mathematics, citing Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, whose manuscripts may have been lost.

3- Physics. He made great achievements in it, chiefly in optics, as his book of that name, written in 1021, attests. It was edited by Dr. Abdelhamid Sabra. The book has seven treatises:

Treatise one: on how vision occurs in general. It has several chapters: the opening of the book, the properties of sight, an inquiry into the properties of lights, how light shines between the eye and the light source, the structure of the eye, how vision occurs, the uses of the organs of sight, and the causes of the things without which vision cannot be complete.

Treatise two: on the errors of sight in what it perceives directly and their causes, in several chapters: the opening of the treatise, distinguishing the lines of the ray, how each of the particular properties perceived by the sense of sight is perceived, and distinguishing the eye's perception of visible objects.

Treatise three: on the errors of sight in what it perceives directly and their causes, in several chapters: the opening of the treatise, preliminaries needed to explain the errors of sight, the causes for which the eye is prone to error, distinguishing the errors of sight, how errors arise from sensation alone, how errors arise in recognition, and how errors arise from inference.

Treatise four: on how sight perceives by reflection from polished bodies, with the following chapters: the opening of the treatise, that the forms of visible things are reflected from polished bodies, how forms are reflected from polished bodies, and that what the eye perceives in polished bodies is perception of visible objects by reflection.

Treatise five: on the positions of images, which are the forms seen in polished bodies, that is, the eye's perception of visible objects by reflection.

Treatise six: on the errors of sight in what it perceives by reflection and their causes, with the following chapters: the opening of the treatise, the errors of sight arising from reflection, errors in plane mirrors, errors in convex spherical mirrors, errors in convex cylindrical mirrors, errors in concave spherical mirrors, errors in concave cylindrical mirrors, and errors in concave conical mirrors.

Treatise seven: on how sight perceives by refraction through transparent bodies whose transparency differs from that of air, with the following chapters: the opening of the treatise, that light passes through transparent bodies along straight lines and bends if it meets a body whose transparency differs from that of the body it is in, how light bends in transparent bodies, that what is perceived through transparent bodies of different transparency from the medium of the eye, when oblique to the normals to their surfaces, is perceived by refraction, images, how the eye perceives visible objects by refraction, and the errors of sight arising from refraction.

Ibn al-Haytham's notes on light

The nature of light: in his time there were two hypotheses about the nature of light, which he presents thus. The first: light is fiery heat emitted from self-luminous bodies; when it shines on a dense body it heats it, and when it is reflected from a concave mirror and gathers at one point it burns whatever is there. (In modern terms, light is energy moving from one form to another, lost and gained.) The second: the light of a self-luminous body is a "substantial form", and this is limited to self-luminous bodies. This is more a description than a definition.

Ibn al-Haytham did not favour one over the other. He did say that light is a subtle material body made of rays with lengths and widths; every ray, whatever it is, has a definite width. He calls these rays "ropes of light". They are emitted by luminous bodies and travel in straight directions as they pass through transparent media, and can be reflected and refracted. Transparent bodies are those through which light passes; this does not mean thinness, but the state water, air or crystal is in.

"Ibn al-Haytham's achievement in optics, compared with the Greek and Arabic mathematical writings before him, shows at first glance two prominent features: breadth and reform" (Encyclopedia vol. 2, p. 843). He dealt with various fields, as his writings show. His reforming programme led him to take up the different questions separately. His basic work in this reform was the separation, for the first time in the history of the science, between the conditions of the propagation of light and the conditions of seeing objects. Optics came to include two parts: a theory of vision tied to the physiology of the eye and the psychology of perception, and a theory of light to which geometrical optics and physical optics are tied.

The Book of Optics begins by rejecting the idea and doctrine of visual rays emitted from the eye and defends the intromission doctrine of the forms of visible things, siding with philosophers and contemporaries such as Ibn Sina. But he differs from them in that he does not regard the forms the eye sees as wholes ("universals") emitted from the visible object under the influence of light; rather, he regards them as forms that can be analysed into their elements, meaning a ray is emitted from every point of the visible object towards the eye. The eye is thus a simple instrument of sight.

He gives five conditions of vision:

1- The visible object must be luminous itself or lit by another light source.

2- The object must face the eye, so that every point of it can be joined to the eye by a straight line.

3- The medium between the object and the eye must be transparent, with no opaque obstacle.

4- The object must be more opaque than this medium.

5- The object must be of a size suited to the acuity of sight.

If these conditions are not met, vision is impossible. As for the conditions of light's existence and propagation, he states:

1- Light exists independently of vision and outside us.

2- Light moves very fast, but not instantaneously or suddenly.

3- Light loses intensity of glare in proportion to its distance from the source.

4- The light of a source is essential, and the light of an illuminated body is secondary or transient. Both spread over surrounding bodies, enter transparent media and light opaque bodies, which in turn send out light.

5- Light spreads from every point of a luminous or illuminated body along straight lines in transparent media in all directions. These lines are parallel or intersecting, and the lights do not merge in either case.

6- Reflected or refracted lights travel in straight lines in particular directions.

It is clear that these conditions are not related to vision. (Encyclopedia, 845–847)

Reflection of light: Ibn al-Haytham explained the phenomenon of reflection and reached the observation that the angle of reflection from polished bodies equals the angle of incidence. He also dealt with unpolished surfaces with many pores, whose parts are scattered and not cohesive: part of the light enters the pores and is lost, and the rest is reflected scattered.

The speed of light: Ibn al-Haytham may have been the first to conclude that light has a finite speed and needs time to travel from one place to another. In his words: "If the hole is covered and then the cover is lifted, and the light reaches from the hole to the opposite body, it can only be in time, even if it escapes the senses." (Optics, Treatise Two).

Refraction of light: in Ibn al-Haytham's view light bends when passing from one medium to another because its speed differs in the two media: the speed of light in a rarer transparent medium is higher than in a denser one.

Some of his experiments and researches on light

We mentioned that Ibn al-Haytham refined the scientific (experimental) method, so it is no wonder he applied his method himself. Among his experiments and researches on light:

1- An experiment to prove that light travels in straight lines. The experiment is simple. He made a small hole in a screen between himself and sunlight. Then he took a flexible tube, put one end on the hole and looked into the other end, moving the tube to different angles with the screen. He found he saw the light only when the tube coincided with a straight line passing through the hole.

2- A study of shadow. Shadows of bodies on the ground occur when these bodies stand between the light shining on them and the dense bodies facing them. He attributes this to light travelling in straight lines and striking the intervening body, so it does not reach the part of the dense body lying on the straight line formed by the light source and the body. He also distinguished between shadow (which he called darkness) and penumbra (which he called shadow). His study may be among the beginnings of photometry.

3- Light entering holes. Ibn al-Haytham realised that light enters holes in a cone, with the plane of the hole cutting the cone. He proved that a body luminous in itself or by another shines from every point on its surface. He did his experiments on incidental lights such as moonlight and self-luminous lights such as sunlight.

4- The dark chamber with a hole. He used the entry of light through a hole to make a dark chamber with a hole, which he called the dark house. He observed the formation of images of the luminous objects standing outside the chamber, and perhaps realised the images are inverted. The narrower the hole, the more precise and clear the image. He found dimensions and conditions for this hole so that the image is formed accurately. From the figure:

HR : AB = CD : EC

where AB is the luminous object and rays from it pass through the hole HR. Two rays leave point A: one touches H and meets the paper screen at E, the other touches R and meets the paper screen at A′. From point B on the luminous object two rays leave: one touches H and meets the paper screen at T, and the other touches R and meets the screen at E.

It is clear that the image of point A on the screen is EA′ and the image of point B is ET. Each image lies tangent to the other, without overlapping. This happens if the following condition holds:

HR : AB = ED : EC

He gave various practical examples, among them the image of the crescent Sun during an eclipse. He showed the image of the whole by geometrical calculations and descriptive drawings, all indicating that the image is inverted.

This is the beginning of the camera (perhaps the origin of the word is "qamara"). Ibn al-Haytham is thus its inventor, the camera obscura, as George Sarton confirms in the scientific encyclopaedia.

5- On colours and the rainbow. Ibn al-Haytham erred in regarding colour as something existing in itself, distinct from light. But he noticed that white is a mixture of colours. To prove it, he made a spinning wheel of colours and turned it fast. He also failed to explain the colours of the rainbow as resulting from reflection of light and not refraction, as Kamal al-Din al-Farisi later showed. Kamal al-Din al-Farisi (1267–1319) explained Ibn al-Haytham's ideas in his (manuscript) "Revision of the Optics for those of sight and insight", and then published them.

6- The eye. Ibn al-Haytham describes four layers of the eye: the white fatty layer, the white of the eye called the conjunctiva, which is most of the eye; the uvea, that is, the pupil, a small hollow coloured sphere; the cornea, the tough transparent layer covering the front of the eye, whose outer surface is spherical and convex while its inner surface is spherical and concave; and finally the crystalline lens, a white, moist sphere of cohesive moisture. He gave details that differ from modern science, but his overall description of the eye seems acceptable. Max Meyerhof said of this: "Ibn al-Haytham was the first to arrange the parts of the eye and draw them with perfect clarity. He gave its parts names that Western medicine took from him, such as cornea and retina." (Legacy of Islam, cited in Ali al-Daffa's "Pure Sciences in the Islamic Eras", p. 316).

7- His theory of vision. Before him the hypotheses were those of emission and of intromission. Ibn al-Haytham formulated a complete theory of vision, the first in the history of science. While interested in the eye's anatomy, he also treated it as an instrument of sight. He used the anatomy of the eye to present his theory of the ray passing from the point of the visible object to the eye, through the centre of the sphere (the eye), and then undergoing refraction because it is not along the radii of the sphere. This led him to consider the layers of the eye as parts of concentric spheres.

His theory rests on vision occurring by light coming from every point of the visible object to the eye. It enters the cornea, passes through the pupil's opening to the aqueous humour, and then to the crystalline humour. He claims the lens is the first organ of the eye's layers that the entering light meets. Therefore any damage to the parts of the eye prevents light reaching the lens and causes blindness; and any damage to the lens abolishes sensation of light and leads to blindness. A question arises here: how does sight perceive these objects distinctly, not mixed together, at one time? His answer is very clever, through the following hypothesis: the eye perceives images of objects distinctly and unmixed because each point on the visible object has a corresponding point on the surface of the lens specialised in sensing its image. Sensation occurs from the ray coming from the point on the object's surface to its corresponding point on the lens surface. The lens senses only straight rays, so the rays must extend along the directions perpendicular to the eye's layers, passing through the centre of sight.

One can imagine a cone whose apex is the centre of the visible object and whose base is the pupil, extending outward from the opening. If it meets a visible object, the lights from the points of the object along the straight lines forming the cone do not suffer refraction, so the lens senses only them. The order in which these rays fall on the front of the lens follows the order of the points of the visible object. This explanation is acceptable in modern science in general. Ibn al-Haytham cites the free fall of bodies in straight lines to support it. And what of objects outside the cone of rays, which the eye still sees and perceives? He demonstrates their existence thus: "If a person holds his index finger by his lower eyelid, deliberately keeping its upper surface parallel to the surface of his sight relative to the forehead, he sees his finger. All these positions are plain and unambiguous: they are outside the cone of rays." (Mustafa Nazif's study, p. 234). This drives him to correct his earlier theory so that perception also occurs by refracted lights. The rays of the cone mentioned above are refracted in the layers of the eye to meet at the lens, except the axis of the cone, the ray along the perpendicular, which suffers no refraction at the lens until it reaches the surface of the vitreous. He considers that perceiving light without refraction, as with the ray along the perpendicular, is stronger than perceiving it after refraction. So objects inside the cone of rays are perceived by light arriving along the perpendiculars and also by refracted light; hence they are seen more clearly than objects outside the cone, which are perceived only by refraction.

Sensation occurs only along the perpendiculars issuing from the centre of sight. The rays meet on the lens forming the image of the object, which stays a moment, then passes to the optic nerve through the opening of the nerve and on to the brain. There the image from each eye is superimposed on the other, forming a stereoscopic image by which the brain perceives colour, the order of points and shape. This means he refers to the nerves of both eyes crossing in the brain. He argues for this: if the right side of the brain is hurt the left eye is disabled, and if the left side is hurt the right eye is disabled.

There are undoubtedly errors in his theory and in his description of the eye. His theory of vision is not exactly the truth, but lies between how insects see and how humans do. The radius of the cornea's surfaces is not greater than that of the uvea; the front surface of the lens is not parallel to the surfaces of the cornea; the geometric axis (axis of symmetry) does not pass through the centre of the nerve's opening; and the cornea is not spherical. Even so, his theory is the first of its kind with such seriousness and method, and was an advanced scientific step beyond what came before.

His contribution to mechanics

Ibn al-Haytham's study of light brought him close to the study of mechanics. We summarise his findings as follows:

a- On motion, he stated that motion is of two kinds: natural motion, resulting from a body being affected by its weight, that is by gravity, or what we call free fall, which depends on the body's weight and the distance it falls; and incidental motion, caused by an external agent. A body at rest moves only through an external force. This hints at Newton's first law. He also pointed to the concept of momentum or (kinetic) energy when he linked a body's speed with the distance covered and with its weight in free fall.

b- In analysing the motion of projectiles, he stated that it is made of two parts: a part along the horizontal and a part perpendicular to it. He did his experiments with an iron ball and an iron mirror. If the ball is thrown at the mirror it bounces off the other side at the same angle at which it was thrown. In his words: "The dependence of a moving body's motion on the obstructing body is compounded of motion towards the direction from which the perpendicular to the surface of the obstructing body extends, and of the motion towards which the perpendicular to this perpendicular extends in the surface in which the motion occurs."

c- On the collision of moving bodies with stationary ones, as usual he drew conclusions from his experiments. He distinguished the types of collision and precisely described the change in the body's motion. He mentioned:

1- The collision of an iron ball with hard rock or iron, which for us is elastic collision.

2- The collision of an iron ball with a soft body such as earth or wool, which is inelastic collision.

He calls the stationary body the obstructing body, because it tries to stop the moving body from continuing. The strength of the obstruction is expressed by the property of resistance, so the resistance of iron, for example, is greater than that of earth. This is what we call hardness, measured by the distance of rebound after collision. In his words: "When heavy bodies fall down from a high place and meet at their landing a hard body such as rock or iron, or the like, they are reflected immediately back, and their return is by a strong motion. If they meet at their landing a soft body such as sand in earth or the like, they stick in it and do not return. And if they meet a body partly hard and partly soft, such as plaster or wood, or the like in softness, they return weakly…" (Chapter three of treatise four of the Optics). His resistance quantitatively is our coefficient of restitution. He explains the rebound of an iron ball after hitting a solid body as follows, in his words, slightly adapted: "Since the motion of the iron ball is the resultant of two components of motion, when it hits the solid body (the mirror) the mirror resists the moving body perpendicularly, so the perpendicular component reverses direction while the horizontal component stays constant, unchanged, since nothing obstructs it in its direction. After the collision there are two components, one perpendicular in the opposite direction and one horizontal in the same direction as before. If we suppose the new perpendicular component, opposite to the previous one, is constant in magnitude, the angle at which the ball is reflected equals the angle at which it falls, on the side of the perpendicular."

His contribution to astronomy

Ibn al-Haytham was also an astronomer, and his astronomical writings come to a quarter of his works (33, as Sezgin lists). Only a few have been edited so far. The author's aim, as Langermann notes, was "to combine Aristotelian physics with the applied mathematics of traditional cosmography and optics". Even his main astronomical book, "On the Configuration of the World", he himself classed among works of physics and metaphysics. Kohl and Langermann showed that Peurbach's (1423–1469) new theory of the planets, which had a great effect on Copernicus (1473–1543), Regiomontanus (1436–1476) and Reinhold (1511–1553), is almost a restatement of Ibn al-Haytham's theory.

Although he followed Ptolemy in his picture of the world, his view of the spheres differed: Ibn al-Haytham saw them as real, explicit transparent bodies. Their eccentric motions and the motions of the epicycles were counted as part of the system. His explanation also differs from Aristotle's on the motion of the spheres. As Langermann wrote: "Applying the basic principles shows that the moving body is that which moves itself in its own proper motion… so no mover is needed for the proper motion of the supposed rotating bodies… the system took the place of the divine mover." The Ptolemaic theory, with 393 solid bodies arranged one above another in his book "Al-Iqtisas", spread in Europe and was accepted, especially until Newton's time, through the book of Ibn al-Haytham mentioned above.

Sezgin lists 27 manuscripts of his, none edited (as of 1976), and 6 manuscripts of which Ibn Abi Usaybi'a preserved only the titles.
