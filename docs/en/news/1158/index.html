The astrolabe and the sine quadrant, two remarkable astronomical instruments – Hani Al-Dhulaie

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

The ancients knew instruments for observing the sky and its many bodies, stars, planets, Sun and Moon, and excelled in making many forms of them, since Ptolemy the Greek, who lived around the second century AD, developed the astrolabe, used to observe the positions of stars and compute their motions.

When many Greek, Latin and Indian works were translated into Arabic in the early ninth century AD (third century AH), interest in astronomy spread and the number of professional instrument makers grew, until the astrolabe became, for example, the watch every wealthy person owned to know the time and follow the motion of the heavenly bodies.

The astrolabe is a purely personal instrument, most being made in small portable sizes, yet relatively heavy because they were usually made of brass so as to hang steadily vertical when used.

Prayer time
Al-Sufi, al-Fazari, Maryam al-Asturlabi, al-Biruni, al-Zarqali and others excelled in making this instrument, whose applications exceeded a hundred, among them knowing the time of day, computing prayer times, sunrise, sunset and noon, the positions of the planets in the sky, the times of rising and setting of stars, the ascendants from the lunar mansions, the times of morning and evening twilight, the latitude of a place, the longitudes and latitudes of stars relative to the ecliptic, and much more.

But what most attracted and worried those scholars was calculating when the afternoon (asr) prayer begins, which does not depend on a sunset, sunrise or noon event but on the length of the shadow, which changes every day and over the year.

So they developed a part of geometric mathematics and coined the term "zill al-tamam" (cotangent), which al-Biruni described in detail in his book "Istiy'ab al-Wujuh al-Mumkina fi San'at al-Asturlab", where he spoke of the sine quadrant (al-rub' al-mujayyab) and drew on it the first and second asr arcs by those developed equations, and many other arcs describing many other applications.

A dazzling instrument
The sine quadrant is a quarter of a circle that can be a separate piece or part of the astrolabe, and is in itself a dazzling astronomical instrument for its simplicity and the many computations it offers: it can work out for us many mathematical operations for geometry without effort, such as the values of sine, cosine and tangent. It is meant for measuring the angles of altitude and depression of bodies, and with it one can easily compute the height of a building or mountain by measuring two angles a set distance apart, as well as observe the altitude of the Sun through its two sighting holes; one can compute the time of the Sun's noon and its declination.

It can also compute the qibla direction very accurately for countries in Asia, Europe and Africa, not to mention fixing the Shafi'i and Hanafi asr times for every day of the year.

The astrolabe, truly regarded as the computer of its day, consists of the rete, representing the map of the sky with its stars and fixed bodies that turn with the Earth's rotation, rising and setting, with the name of a bright star engraved near each sharp pointed tip.

The pointer or rule placed over the rete fixes the time and date, using the map on the back of the astrolabe, the main reference for fixing the Sun's position in the sky for a given date of the year, with another rule, called the alidade, responsible for determining those data; the base holds a number of plates, one for each city.

The abjad numeral system
On each plate are drawn all the main circles of the sky, such as the equator, the horizon, the ecliptic, the dawn arc and the circles of altitude above the horizon; the pole star, representing the axis of rotation, is also drawn, which is the astrolabe's own axis turning about the celestial pole, its altitude above the northern horizon equalling the latitude of the place, which is why the plates differ from each other.

The Arabs excelled in drawing the maps and nets of the astrolabe, in Arabic scripts dominated by Kufic and Maghrebi, numbering them by the abjad (hisab al-jummal) system, counting with letters in alphabetical order (abjad hawwaz hutti kalaman…).

The first nine letters represent the nine digits (1–9), followed by the letter ya for ten, kaf twenty, lam thirty, mim forty, and so on up to one thousand (1000); the two letters (kaf, ha), for example, mean 25. On the astrolabe pictured, made by the Moroccan astronomer Muhammad ibn Ahmad Mursil, he states he finished making it in the year (sheen-ra-lam-ba), equivalent by Maghrebi abjad counting to 1232 AH.

Hardly any museum in the world lacks an astrolabe whose markings are engraved in Arabic letters, and most names of the bright stars on this distinctive instrument are of Arabic origin, such as Aldebaran, Deneb, Rasalhague, Rigel and others, which shows the high standing Arab astronomy had in the flourishing eras of Islam and the eras and generations after, who inherited this civilisation.

Hani Al-Dhulaie
