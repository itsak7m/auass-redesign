The Sharjah Planetarium

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Marwan Shwaiki,

Director of the Sharjah Planetarium and President of the Arab Planetarium Society

However well we describe it, nothing replaces seeing it for yourself: such experiences come only from sitting in one of the planetarium's 200 seats, beneath a giant dome screen 18 metres across, with countless stars and beautiful celestial objects twinkling above you, in an hour-long break that captivates the mind and makes you forget everything going on in the outside world!

With an 18-metre screen and more than 200 seats, the planetarium at the Sharjah Academy for Astronomy, Space Sciences and Technology is the largest of its kind in the Arab world, and it is equipped with the latest optical and digital projection systems.

When you buy your ticket and pass through the electronic gate that reads its barcode by laser, you will be amazed as you enter the wide round hall topped by a giant white dome screen. You can sit anywhere, but it is best to choose a seat near the middle of the back rows of the huge hall, to get a complete view that includes the area behind your seat. This is called 360×180-degree viewing: 360 degrees around you and 180 degrees from front to back and from right to left. Before one of the expert staff begins the show, you will surely notice the beautiful red device in the centre of the hall. It is the star projector, one of the most advanced devices found at the centre of the world's planetariums, especially those that show the stars as we see them in the desert, as if real: sharp, bright and twinkling. That is all you need as a visitor to enjoy a star show as if you were lying in the desert on a clear, moonless night.

Projection systems:

The Academy's planetarium runs two parallel projection systems, an optical system and a full-dome digital system, making it a hybrid planetarium that combines the two, which gives it an advantage over planetariums with a single projection system.

Opto-mechanical projection system:

The centrepiece of this system is a Japanese MEGASTAR star projector, together with seven special precision projectors for the seven main celestial bodies: the Sun, the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. They are all run by their own server and connected in precise harmony with the second system. The MEGASTAR projector moves at great speed, simulating the sky at any time and from any place the audience wishes to see. It projects about 10 million separate points of light, mainly stars down to the seventh magnitude, most of which together form a legendary picture of our Milky Way galaxy, along with other faint objects. Under this dome, what most catches the visitor's attention is the Milky Way, rendered as if it were truly real.

Full-dome digital projection system:

This system consists of seven projection channels with seven advanced DLP digital projectors, run by 7 servers that together produce one complete image across the whole dome. With this modern system, all the details of the constellations can be shown, such as their boundaries, their stick figures and their ancient mythological shapes, turning the sky into a great dramatic stage that becomes ever more thrilling and moving with accompanying music. In addition, the system shows astronomy and space documentaries and close-up views of the planets: you can, for example, glide through space over Saturn's beautiful rings as if aboard a spacecraft.

The main simulator: Uniview

The Uniview simulation software links all the systems above seamlessly, putting in the presenter's hands what is effectively the control panel of a spaceship and a scientific console for the sky and for moving through space. It is a simulator that works as a machine for travelling through space and time: with it, the presenter can show the stars and constellations and draw all the lines, grids and elements of the celestial sphere, and even of the Earth and other planets. The presenter can take visitors on a journey through space to see the International Space Station up close as if about to dock with it, and many satellites and space telescopes. And it does not stop there: visitors can continue through the planets of the solar system, leave our Milky Way galaxy to see it up close before departing, travel through the Local Group of galaxies, and head deeper and farther, to the edge of the universe.
