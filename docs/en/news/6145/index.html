How do we control the danger of space debris, and what are the prospects for solving it?

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Space debris is classed as a major problem that could put space travel at risk if it is not solved in the right way.

Over the past decade humans' eagerness to reach Mars and beyond has been plain, as private and state-funded organisations plan to intensify our flights into space and build habitable habitats over the next few decades. This is tied to our ability to leave our planet safely and smoothly. But our dream remains fraught with risk, because of the accumulation of space debris floating around our planet and its threat to the lives of astronauts on the International Space Station.

Space waste is not limited to large pieces: our planet is surrounded by pieces of debris orbiting at speeds of up to about 28,163.52 kilometres per hour, about 10 times faster than a bullet. Their number reaches 128 million pieces between one millimetre and one centimetre in size, 900,000 pieces between one centimetre and 10 centimetres, in addition to 34,000 pieces larger than 10 centimetres, according to European Space Agency estimates.

The US site Wired quoted Jack Bacon, a scientist at the US National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), as saying that "a collision between a piece of aluminium and a piece of space debris about 10 centimetres in size is equivalent to detonating 7 kilograms of dynamite, and the problem is not easy to deal with."

The danger of space debris stems from its ability to cut cables or damage spacecraft windows, and it may cause the death or injury of astronauts.

Strange objects floating around our planet

The accumulation of space debris is the result of sixty years of space activity, and contains remnants of communications equipment, disabled satellites, burnt-out rocket pieces, lost tools, and particles produced by explosions and collisions.

We may also come across small flakes of paint estimated in the millions, parts of launch vehicles, solidified fluids from rocket engines, and stranger still, floating gloves, ammonia canisters and urine waste, as well as the ashes of Gene Roddenberry, creator of the Star Trek series.

Stuart Grey, Professor of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering at the University of Strathclyde in Scotland, said in a special interview with Mostaqbal Observatory: "We can track about 20,000 pieces of space debris, even those larger than 10 centimetres. More than half of these objects are debris resulting from random collisions. A quarter are spacecraft, including roughly 1,500 active ones, and the remainder is no more than rocket debris and the waste of space missions. The overall picture of this debris is very worrying, as the total mass of the debris in orbit is about 8,000 metric tonnes of human-made material."

Monitoring space debris

NASA's Orbital Debris Program Office monitors space debris, in cooperation with several governments and private institutions, to reach a better understanding of it, count it and archive it, using a large set of telescopes around the world, and tracking small pieces of less than one centimetre using ground radars.

Containing the problem

It may take decades before we achieve any workable progress in making launches debris-free. Even if we stopped launching objects into space, things would not improve, as that debris would keep colliding with itself, which multiplies the problem.

The Orbital Debris Program Office indicated, in a computer simulation, that current debris will increase from two to 20 times over two centuries, which requires global action to contain the problem, according to Interesting Engineering.

Future solutions

NASA and a number of organisations around the world have proposed a series of solutions to ease the space debris problem; here are the most prominent

The space sling

Engineers from Texas A&M University designed a simple, low-cost idea, which they called the space sling (or space broom), whose job is to capture objects and carry them towards our atmosphere, using that momentum to launch towards another group of debris, saving fuel.

Space debris removal system

Developed by Raytheon BBN Technologies with NASA funding, its job is to puff air into a lower orbit and place it in the orbit of the space debris, which increases the drag on the debris so that it leaves orbit.

Space harpooning

In 2014 the idea of space harpooning emerged, targeting space debris in a polar orbit at an altitude between 800 and 1,000 kilometres. There are various means of capturing space waste, including harpoons, magnets, giant nets and robotic arms.

Space sails

A cube-shaped sail uses drag to push space debris into lower orbits.

Recycling

We could look at this space debris in a different way: most of its material is expensive and used in making rockets and satellites. Should we look at this space waste as resources of raw materials not yet exploited?

Grey told Mostaqbal Observatory: "These objects are made of special materials with a very high production cost, and merely thinking of them as a ready-to-use source is an intriguing idea, but the obstacle to applying the option is the great effort needed to collect and harness the resources."

A net

Scientists and engineers from NASA's Advanced Concepts and Innovation department proposed a programme that uses a spacecraft thinner than a hair, able to wrap around space debris like a net.

Laser fragmentation

Others proposed using laser beams to break up space debris.

A space cleaner robot

In December 2019 the European Space Agency formally approved funding for the ClearSpace-1 mission, the first mission dedicated to cleaning up space debris, at a cost of about 133 million dollars. The agency's plan is to begin in 2025, when a team from the Swiss start-up ClearSpace launches a robotic spacecraft into space. The space robot will grab with its four arms a piece called Vespa, a 100-kilogram part left by a European Space Agency launch vehicle called Vega, and pull it down to Earth, so that it and Vespa burn up in the atmosphere.

The harpoon and the net

Researchers designed the first spacecraft to capture space debris, called RemoveDebris, which relies on a harpoon, a net, a vision-based navigation system and a sail. Its idea is to fire a giant net to capture debris, or to aim a harpoon to catch waste. The spacecraft is under testing after being shipped to the International Space Station aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket.
