New study: the seven TRAPPIST-1 planets are alike!

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

By Eng. Majed Abu Zahra

A new study has found that the seven Earth-sized planets around the star TRAPPIST-1 are all very similar in composition, but are probably quite different from Earth.

The seven planets orbiting the nearby star TRAPPIST-1 were discovered by the Spitzer Space Telescope in 2017, at a distance of at least 40 light years. Details about them have been hard to obtain, but astronomers have wondered: are they like Earth? Are some like Venus? Do any of them have clouds or even surface water?

On 22 January 2021 astronomers added another piece to the TRAPPIST-1 puzzle when they published a paper in the Planetary Science Journal detailing the composition of these worlds. The team found that they are made of similar materials, yet quite unlike the planets of our solar system.

TRAPPIST-1 is a red dwarf, the most common type of star in our Milky Way galaxy. Three of its planets lie within the star's habitable zone, where liquid water could exist on a planet's surface.

At present, astronomers feel it is unlikely that water will be found on the surfaces of the three inner TRAPPIST-1 planets, and if the four outer planets have surface water, it is not in large amounts.

This finding contradicts the earlier belief among astronomers that low-density planets like these should be rich in water, and it raises questions about how Earth-like and habitable the seven planets could be.

By combining computer models of planetary interiors from the universities of Bern and Zurich with planetary atmosphere models being developed at the University of Geneva, scientists were able to assess the water content of the seven TRAPPIST-1 planets with unprecedented precision.

The interior and atmosphere models show that the three inner planets of the TRAPPIST-1 system are probably dry, and that the four outer planets contain no more than a few per cent of water, perhaps in liquid form on their surfaces.

Now let us think for a moment about the eight planets of our solar system. They vary widely in density: the gas giants and ice giants (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune) are less dense, and the rocky planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars) are denser.

Because the seven TRAPPIST-1 planets are known to share relatively similar densities, the researchers faced a dilemma when comparing them with Earth: if any of them really has surface water, astronomers' density estimates would have to change.

So at the start of the study the researchers set out to determine whether the low density of the seven planets is due to water or to their interior composition, either of which would decide whether the planets are fully or only somewhat like Earth.

According to the study, the outer planets are about 8% less dense than Earth. For this low density to be due to surface water, about 5% of each planet's mass would have to be surface water.

That is a lot by comparison: water makes up only 0.1% of the Earth's mass, so you can see at once that these planets are compositionally quite different from Earth.

Even a few per cent of water is doubtful: the planets most likely hold less than a few per cent of their mass in water, otherwise the similar densities of all seven planets would be an extraordinary coincidence.

To answer this puzzle, the researchers examined the composition of the seven planets, looking for similarities with Earth.

Most rocky planets are thought to be made of metals such as magnesium, iron and nickel, together with sulphur, oxygen and silicon.

Because of the density difference between the seven planets and Earth, the researchers believe the TRAPPIST-1 planets may have an Earth-like composition but in markedly different proportions, for example less iron: while iron makes up 32% of the Earth's total mass, the study suggests iron should make up about 21% of the mass of each TRAPPIST-1 planet.

The low density suggests these planets have a different composition, and therefore a different history, from the rocky planets of our solar system.

To determine each planet's mass, the scientists measured changes in their orbital periods, timing how long each planet takes to orbit TRAPPIST-1 and transit in front of it as seen from Earth.

Whether the differences between Earth and the TRAPPIST-1 planets change the chances of life somewhere in that system is a complex question. While liquid water could point to the possibility of life, other factors help make a planet suitable for life to grow, thrive and survive. On Earth, for example, the magnetic field protects our planet and its life from high-energy particles from the Sun, and our atmosphere holds enough oxygen and carbon, along with the other gases needed for animal life and photosynthesis, and keeps surface temperatures habitable.

Despite these challenges, life on the TRAPPIST-1 planets is not ruled out, as the link to habitability is not yet clear. The planets' structure may affect their ability to form plate tectonics and a magnetic field, among other possible effects; these aspects of the Earth affect the existence and evolution of life on it, so many questions still await answers.

The study used data collected by the Spitzer Space Telescope since the system was discovered more than four years ago: between 2016 and 2020, when the telescope was switched off and stopped collecting data, Spitzer logged 1,075 hours of observing time on TRAPPIST-1. Although Spitzer has been retired, that is not a permanent end to the study of TRAPPIST-1.

If the James Webb Space Telescope is launched and operated successfully later this year, 2021, there are plans to continue observations with it to try to probe the planets' atmospheres using spectroscopy and to improve the data on the planets' masses.
