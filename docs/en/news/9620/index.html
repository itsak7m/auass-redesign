Science news · 18 January 2021

The most distant quasar yet discovered

Translated from the Arabic original. اقرأ النص الأصلي بالعربية

Prepared by Eng. Majed Abu Zahra

A team of astronomers announced the discovery of a quasar about 13.03 billion light years from Earth, named J031343.84-180636.4, or J0313-1806 for short. It is 1,000 times brighter than the Milky Way and powered by a supermassive black hole 1.6 billion times larger than the Sun. The existence of such a huge black hole only 670 million years after the Big Bang is a major challenge to theoretical models of the growth of massive black holes, and the results were published in the Astrophysical Journal.

Quasars are believed to be caused by supermassive black holes devouring the material around them, such as gas or even whole stars, producing a swirl of extremely hot matter known as an "accretion disc" that orbits the black hole. Because of the huge energies, quasars are among the brightest sources in the universe, and often outshine the galaxies in which they reside.

Although quasar J0313-1806 is only 20 million light years away from the previous record holder, the quasar ULAS J134208.10 + 092838.61, it contains a huge black hole twice the other's mass, and this represents major progress in cosmology, because it provides the strongest constraint yet on the formation of black holes in the early universe.

This is the first evidence of how a supermassive black hole affects the galaxy around it. In addition, observations of less distant galaxies indicate that this must happen, but we have not seen it happening this early in the universe.

The most distant quasars are crucial for understanding how early black holes formed and for understanding cosmic reionisation, the last major transition phase of our universe.

Once the scientists confirmed its identity as a quasar, they obtained high-quality spectra from the Keck Observatory in Hawaii and the Gemini North telescope in Hawaii to measure the mass of the central supermassive black hole.

Measuring the spectral lines that arise from the gas surrounding the quasar's accretion disc makes it possible to determine the black hole's mass and study how its rapid growth affects its environment.

For such distant quasars the most important spectral lines are redshifted to near-infrared wavelengths, and Keck's NIRES spectrograph is an excellent instrument for these surveys.

The huge mass of the black hole J0313-1806 at such an early time in the universe's history rules out two theoretical models of how these objects form.

The first model says that individual massive stars explode as supernovae and collapse into black holes, which then merge into larger black holes; in the second model, dense clusters of stars collapse to become a supermassive black hole.

However, in both cases the process takes a long time to produce a massive black hole like the one in J0313-1806, and this tells us that this black hole's seed was formed by a different mechanism.

In this case it is a mechanism involving huge amounts of cold primordial hydrogen gas collapsing directly into a black-hole-forming seed.

Observation data of quasar J0313-1806 from the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) revealed puzzling details about the quasar's host galaxy, which is forming new stars at a rate more than 200 times that of our Milky Way.

This rate of star formation is relatively high in galaxies of similar ages, and indicates that the quasar's host galaxy is growing very rapidly.

The brightness of quasar J0313-1806 indicates that its supermassive black hole swallows the equivalent of 25 suns every year, and the energy released by this rapid feeding is probably powering a strong flow of ionised gas that has been seen moving at about 20% of the speed of light.

Such outflows are believed to be what eventually halts star formation in the galaxy, and those supermassive black holes are believed to have been the reason many large galaxies stopped forming stars at some point.

"Quenching" was subsequently observed at later times, but so far it is not known how this process began early in the universe's history.

Quasar J0313-1806 is the first evidence that "quenching" may have been happening at very early times in the universe's history, and this process will also leave the black hole with nothing to eat and halt its growth.
