Astronomy knowledge

The solar system

Mercury is one of the planets nearest the Sun. The ancient Greeks saw it as a bright body just after sunset and called it by the name of the god Hermes, and saw it again just before sunrise as a bright body and called it by the name of the god Apollo, thinking they were two different bodies. A long time passed before they realised it was a single body, and they called it the messenger of the gods (Hermes, whom the Romans equated with Mercury) because it is close to the Sun. Mercury, which circles in the care of its mother the Sun in an inner orbit as seen by an observer on Earth, does not rise very high in the sky. It is always seen near the horizon and sets after only a short while. For this reason many astronomers were never able to observe it with the naked eye. Nor can the planet be observed against a dark sky, because it sets soon after the Sun. Scientists therefore study it by observing it during daylight hours using large telescopes, when the planet is above the horizon and the Sun is also high above the horizon.

Exploring Mercury:

I have often felt sorry that scientists neglected this planet, so much so that some called it the forgotten planet. Since the uncrewed spacecraft Mariner 10 passed close to the planet in 1974–1975, no spacecraft had been sent to it, until NASA launched the new spacecraft Messenger in 2004 to head for this planet and reach it in 2011, beginning a new phase of study. Mariner 10 was launched on 13/11/1973 from Cape Canaveral on a journey to explore the inner planets. The aim of the flight was to visit Venus and then go into orbit around the Sun, crossing in front of Mercury while at its perihelion, while at its aphelion it would lie in an orbit between the Earth and Venus. It passed close to Mercury only three times: the first flyby was on 29/3/1974, then on 21/9/1974, and the third and last on 16/3/1975. During these flybys it was close to the planet and managed to send us a huge number of wonderful pictures of the planet's surface.

The spacecraft had been placed in an orbit around the Sun such that it completed its orbit in 176 days, returning past the planet about every six months. But it passed in front of the planet over the same region each time, so the cameras on this mission covered no more than 45% of the planet's surface area. After this cycle contact with the spacecraft was lost on 24/3/1975, and it remained in its orbit around the Sun with no way to reach it or bring it back to life.

The orbit of Mercury:

This small planet, whose diameter does not exceed 4,879 km, slightly larger than our Moon, orbits far from the Sun at an average distance of 58 million km (0.38 astronomical units) in an elliptical orbit in which it is at perihelion 46 million km away and at aphelion nearly 70 million km away. We see that the planet comes very close to the Sun at perihelion, where its speed increases, and slows down at aphelion. This orbit deviates slightly from the orbits of the other planets, which lie roughly in the plane of the Sun's equator (the ecliptic). The planet's orbit is inclined to this plane by about seven degrees, and the only body that resembles it in this orbital anomaly is the dwarf planet Pluto. The greatest distance of the planet from the Earth is about 219 million km and the least about 92 million km. Because it is so close to the Sun, its greatest elongation in the sky ranges up to 28 degrees, at which the planet can be seen just before sunrise on the eastern horizon, when the planet is at its greatest distance from the Sun on the western side and is then at aphelion, called greatest western elongation, and 18 degrees, at which the planet can be seen shortly after sunset on the western horizon, when the planet is at its greatest distance from the Sun on the eastern side and is then at perihelion, called greatest eastern elongation.

Because this planet's orbit is inside the Earth's, the planet may pass in front of the Sun's disc as it orbits, which is known as a transit. The first transit of the planet in front of the Sun's disc was observed on 7/11/1631 by Kepler. By following and observing the dates of the planet's transits across the Sun's disc up to the present, it has been noticed that this occurs only in May and November. November transits occur at intervals of 7, 13 and 33 years, while May transits occur every 13 and 33 years, and the last three transits were in 1999, 2003 and 2006.

The May transit occurs when the planet is at aphelion, far from the Sun, while the November transit occurs when the planet is at perihelion, that is, close to the Sun (the more frequent case, occurring in a ratio of 7:3). But what distinguishes the May transit is that it is longer: the planet continues to move in front of the Sun's disc for nine hours, during which one can watch the planet move across the Sun's disc as a dark spot while it travels from west to east in its orbit.

Mercury takes 88 Earth days to orbit the Sun once (0.24 Earth years). As for its daily rotation on its axis, that has a history from before the Mariner 10 probe was sent to the planet. The first telescopic study of the planet was by William Herschel in the eighteenth century. Then Schiaparelli drew the first map of it from study between 1881 and 1889, in which he observed dark spots on its surface and kept seeing these spots and tracking their motion. He attributed the reason for seeing the same features to the planet turning the same face to the Earth when at perihelion, and found that the planet's rotation on its axis equals its revolution around the Sun (synchronous rotation), so that it always turns the same face to the Sun and the same face to the Earth. It takes 88 Earth days to go round the Sun, so it would have one face always in daylight facing the Sun and the other half in night on the side away from the Sun. It stayed that way until 1962, when Howard and his colleagues in Michigan studied the infrared radiation reflected from the dark side facing away from the Sun and found it warmer than it could be if in permanent night. The planet must therefore rotate with respect to the Sun so that the Sun falls on all its faces in turn, night and day.

In 1965 there was decisive confirmation that the planet's day and year are not equal, after the scientists Pettengill and Dyce in Puerto Rico used the radio telescope to study Mercury. Waves of known frequency were sent to the planet; they struck it and bounced off its surface back towards the source, that is, the Earth, and were received by the same radio telescope, which was considered a receiver of the waves ten minutes after it had been a transmitter of them. When the returned waves were studied it was found that the planet rotates on its axis and is not fixed, facing the Sun with one face, as had been believed. If we assume the planet is fixed and always faces the Sun with one face, the wavelength of the returned wave would equal the wavelength of the wave emitted from the Earth. But if the planet moves and changes its direction towards the Sun in succession, the waves returned towards the Earth will differ according to the part that reflected them, depending on the Doppler effect. A wave reflected from a part moving towards the Earth has a high frequency (shorter wavelength) and is shifted towards blue, while the part moving away from the Earth returns a wave of low frequency (longer wavelength), a shift towards red. The results of studying the waves actually reflected from the planet matched the latter expectation. Applying mathematical relations to these results, the planet's rotation period came to 58.7 days, a very slow rotation, so Mercury completes a full rotation in front of the Sun and the succession of night and day occurs on it. It rotates on its axis like the Earth, from west to east, so the Sun rises over Mercury from the east and sets in the west. The Italian scientist Giuseppe Colombo noticed that this daily rotation of the planet on its axis in 59 days equals roughly two thirds of its annual revolution around the Sun in 88 days, that is, when the planet has rotated three full times it has gone round the Sun twice, so simply every three Mercurian days equal two Mercurian years, and thus the harmony between Mercury's annual and daily motion is in a ratio of 3:2. There is a third strange motion of this planet, the solar day, which is the time from sunrise to the next sunrise at the same place on the planet, equal to two Mercurian years or 176 Earth days. Scientists attributed the reason for this harmony to the planet's elliptical orbit with its high eccentricity e = 0.21, which changes its speed in its orbit, and also to the slowness of the planet's rotation under the Sun's strong gravitational pull, and the absence of any moons also helped this slowness.

Heat and atmosphere on Mercury:

Mercury goes round the Sun upright in its orbit so that exactly half the planet faces the Sun in daytime and the other half is in night, so a huge temperature contrast occurs on its surface unmatched among the planets of the solar system. During the day the Sun's rays are bright and direct on it, raising the temperature on this side to about 700 kelvin (427 °C), and when it turns so that this face is on the side away from the Sun it enters pitch darkness and intense cold, with its surface temperature reaching 100 K (−183 °C), which makes the contrast great, reaching about 600 K between the freezing, bitterly cold night and the blazing, scorching day.
The reason for this extreme surface temperature contrast on Mercury is attributed to the almost complete absence of an atmosphere, as Mercury is considered devoid of gases. Scientists resorted to several means to study the presence or absence of an atmosphere. The first was to watch for the formation of any form of clouds or haze floating on the planet's surface; the second was spectral analysis during the day to show whether light could be absorbed by atmospheric components; and the third was studying the reflection of rays off the surface of stars passing near the planet or during an occultation. But none of these studies gave any positive result or sign of any form of atmosphere on it.

Scientists resorted to a fourth method, using an ultraviolet spectrometer on the dark side of the planet and detecting the light emitted by atmospheric components, resembling the aurora on Earth. This method did not determine any results on an atmosphere. When Mariner 10 approached the planet it was able to detect very thin components whose concentration soon changes: the gases hydrogen, helium, sodium and potassium. It is believed that the hydrogen and helium are carried by the solar wind, so their source is the Sun. The sodium and potassium come from the planet's surface and may be due to the solar wind striking the planet's rocks violently and causing erosion and the tearing out of these atoms and their release in very light concentrations around the planet. Other scientists believe the cause is meteorite impacts on the planet's surface. Whatever their source, the absence of an atmosphere makes atmospheric pressure so low as to be nil, estimated at 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ millibar, so the atmosphere is considered nearly a vacuum as we said. The planet's gravity, estimated at 0.4 of Earth's gravity, may also be a factor in the absence of an atmosphere, as this weak gravity cannot hold gases around the planet. And because no gases surround it as an envelope, the reflectivity (albedo) of its surface is very low = 0.1, yet it appears bright because of its closeness to the Sun.

Surface features of Mercury:

When Mariner 10, on its only trip to Mercury, approached the planet and transmitted a set of pictures, everyone was amazed at the strong resemblance between this planet and the Earth's Moon. The reason for this strong resemblance between the two bodies is the existence of this huge number of craters of different diameters but resembling each other closely, which shows that the ages of Mercury and the Moon are undoubtedly close in terms of the history of geological events on their surfaces. The first crater observed was the crater (Kuiper), and many kinds of features were imaged: plains and highlands, valleys, slopes, trenches and chains of hills (scarps) extending across the surface and cutting through some craters, which shows they occurred after the end of the barrage of meteorite impacts that took place on the planet's surface long ago. Some features may have appeared because of the rapid cooling of the planet's surface and the freezing of its crust, like wrinkles. Among the best-known surface features are two hot poles at which the temperature reaches its highest. At the first pole lies the most famous crater, the Caloris basin, whose age is estimated at 4 thousand million years, and which is believed to have formed from a huge impact on the planet's surface in this region. It was given this name to mean heat (calorie), as the heat is at its extreme = 430 °C when this basin is at perihelion and directly facing the Sun. On the exact opposite side of the basin lies a region of highlands and irregular, anomalous terrain covering 360 thousand km² of the planet's area, made up of valleys, hills and mountains reaching 2 km in height, called the "weird terrain", which is believed to have been formed on the opposite side by the waves produced by the impact that caused the Caloris crater.

It is worth noting that Mariner 10 was able to image only half of the Caloris crater, as it lies on the line between night and day, so only the part in daylight was imaged, and from it the diameter of this crater was concluded to be 1,300 km, surrounded by a chain of highlands ranging from 1 to 2 km in height. The features were named after many famous people. Mercury's surface has some distinguishing features not found on the Moon that need deep geological study, such as the observation that ejecta from impact craters do not scatter far from the crater compared with the Moon, because of the planet's gravity, which, despite its smallness, is twice the Moon's, and also that the highlands are less lofty than the Moon's highlands for the same reason. On Mercury's surface there is a feature made of a series of twisting hills extending for 500 km, some reaching 3 km in height, which distinguishes its surface from the Moon's. The surface is characterised by some overlapping craters and regions with a huge number of small craters, and the surface then looks rough in outline. It was noticed that there are no continental plates (plate tectonics) on Mercury, as on the Moon, and perhaps naming the planet "the boring planet" goes back to its great similarity to the Earth's Moon. Studies found that the planet's rocks contain titanium and iron in lower proportions than the Moon's rocks and are of the basaltic type. In 1991 radar images of the planet were taken, and analysing them showed signs of plates of water ice in the planet's north polar region, in an area lying in the shadow of some craters that the cameras of Mariner 10 did not reach. Mass, density and composition: one of the important products of Mariner 10 was calculating Mercury's mass, as it could not be calculated from Earth because of its small size (its diameter is 4,879 km) and because it has no moons. But Mariner 10 was able to calculate its mass, which equals 3.302 × 10²³ kg, a large mass for its small size (0.06 of Earth's mass). It is therefore a very heavy planet (slightly larger than the Moon, but its mass is four times the Moon's). There is no doubt that the planet's great density, reaching 5.43 g/cm³ (very close to Earth's density), indicates a large proportion of iron in its core, indeed forming the greater part of its composition. The iron core is expected to account for 3,600 km of the planet's diameter (equal to 80% of its diameter), that is, nearly half its volume. This iron core is surrounded by a thin layer of rock reaching 600 km in thickness. Perhaps this is the reason the planet Mercury is called the iron planet.

Some astronomers believe its rocky crust was larger, but because of an impact that occurred during this eventful planet's life cycle, a large part of its crust was knocked off, leaving an iron core surrounded by a thin rocky crust. Not only that, but perhaps this explains the planet's anomalous orbit, inclined 7° from the ecliptic line. For this impact to produce this result, the impacting body would have to have a mass equal to 0.2 of the planet's mass and an impact speed equal to 20 km/s. Scientists believe Mercury has been geologically dead for more than four billion years, just like the Moon. The magnetic field: one of the surprising results of studying the Mariner 10 results was the discovery of positive signals from the magnetic-field probes carried on the spacecraft. The spacecraft carried instruments to detect any trace of the planet's magnetic field, and how astonishing the result was. It is believed that the planet, because of its small size, froze quickly and its iron core cooled and solidified long ago, and cannot work as a dynamo and be the source of this magnetic force, which equals 1/100 of the strength of the Earth's magnetic field. It is known that a planet needs a molten core and sufficient rotation speed to produce a magnetic field. It was found that Mercury has a magnetic field similar to the Earth's magnetic field, that is, a compass needle on its surface deflects towards the geographic north pole, and that this field is strong enough to deflect the solar wind. The axis of this magnetic field was found to deviate slightly from the planet's axis of rotation. These results were surprising because, knowing that the Moon, Venus and Mars have no magnetic field, we can say Mercury is unique in this property, so we must explain the source of this force. The first conclusion scientists reached was that this planet's core is still molten and not solidified, but the planet is very slow in its rotation, so this speed is not enough to generate this magnetic force. Venus, for example, is believed to have a liquid core but has no magnetic field because of its slow rotation on its axis. The source of this field is undoubtedly still mysterious and unknown, and we need a convincing explanation.

Venus

Venus is the second planet in distance from the Sun and one of the brightest celestial bodies after the Sun and the Moon. Venus is one of the planets known since prehistory, and the Arabs worshipped it in pre-Islamic times and called it al-'Uzza, while they called the Sun al-Lat. Venus was seen in the morning just before sunrise and was called the morning star, and seen in the evening after sunset and called the evening star, as happened with Mercury. This continued until the sixth century BC, when the picture became clear in minds and it was known to be a single body. It was named after the Greek goddess of love and beauty, Aphrodite, and the Romans called it Venus, goddess of beauty. Among the Babylonians it was known as the goddess of love, Ishtar, while the ancient Chinese called it the beautiful white planet.

The planet's orbit:

Venus orbits the Sun in an orbit that is nearly circular, as its eccentricity is 0.007, which means the difference between its distance from the Sun at aphelion and at perihelion does not differ much, the difference between the two distances being only 1.5 million km. Its average distance from the Sun is 108 million km (0.72 astronomical units). Venus's orbit crosses the plane of the ecliptic (the plane of the Earth's orbit around the Sun) at two points through which the planet passes in front of the Sun as seen from Earth twice a year, the ascending node in December and the descending node in June.

If one of these two nodes happens to occur at inferior conjunction, that is, the Sun, Venus and Earth lie in one straight line, the phenomenon of a transit occurs. The inferior conjunction recurs once every 584 days, but because Venus's orbit is inclined 3.4° to the ecliptic, a Venus transit across the Sun's disc is very rare. The planet passes above or below the ecliptic and so does not pass in front of the Sun's disc. Transits occur in the first week of June or around 10 December because the planet passes through the two nodes at those dates. Transits of Venus across the Sun's disc recur periodically: 8 years, 121.5 years, 8 years, 105.5 years, and so on.

The first to predict transits of Venus across the Sun's disc was Johannes Kepler in 1627, when he said that Venus and Mercury would cross in front of the Sun's disc in 1631, but he died before the event. The transit of Mercury was observed on 7/11/1631, and astronomers could not observe the transit of Venus at that time. The transit of 1639 was observed by two English amateur astronomers, Crabtree and Horrocks. It was followed by the transit of 1761, then 1769, then 1874 and 1882, so transits of Venus have occurred only six times since the invention of the telescope, and with the last transit in 2004 that makes seven. The phenomenon of the transit of Venus was of great importance in the history of astronomy, as Edmond Halley proposed using this phenomenon to calculate the Earth's distance from the Sun by observing from different regions and using parallax. But the results of the observations were disappointing at the time. It is said that al-Farabi observed a transit of Venus in 910 AD, but this claim has not been proven.

The last transit of Venus across the Sun's disc was on 8/6/2004, when thousands of astronomy amateurs in the world, among them members of the Jordanian Astronomical Society, observed this rare event, which had not happened since 1882. Since the Sun's apparent disc is 32 times larger than Venus's apparent disc, observers saw a black spot on the Sun's disc easily using small binoculars, and of course with a filter to protect the eye from the Sun's harmful rays. The next transit is expected, God willing, in 2012.

Venus is an inner planet relative to the Earth so it is not seen through a whole night, as is the case with Mercury, but its visibility period is longer because its greatest elongation ranges between 45° and 47°, which allows it to be seen for a maximum of 3 hours in the best conditions after sunset or before sunrise. The eye cannot miss it, as it is very clearly visible because of its intense brightness: the reflectivity of sunlight off its surface reaches 0.7, which means that more than 70% of the sunlight falling on its surface is reflected, so it is seen sparkling like a diamond on the eastern or western horizon.

The smaller the area of the observed part of its surface, the greater its brightness! One of the planet's strange properties is that the smaller the area of the lit part of the planet's surface as seen by the observer, the greater this planet's brilliance. How is this? Venus, as we mentioned, is an inner planet relative to the Earth. Because its orbit is inside the Earth's orbit, it is seen in different phases just like the Moon. It develops from a crescent to a quarter to a full phase and so on. Galileo had observed these phases with his telescope in 1610 and considered them strong evidence for the correctness of Copernicus's heliocentric theory, as these phases could only occur if the planet orbited the Sun. Edmond Halley in 1721 was the first to notice that the planet's brightness and brilliance increase when the size of the lit part of its surface decreases.

We know that the full Moon is brighter than the crescent, but this does not apply to Venus. When the planet is full it is at superior conjunction (Earth–Sun–Venus), and the planet is then at the very far end of its orbit from the Earth, 1.7 astronomical units (257 million km) away, and because of its distance its brightness is low. When it begins to move towards the Earth, its lit disc begins to shrink, but as it approaches the Earth its brightness increases until it reaches its greatest elongation (eastern or western), when it is at the quarter phase. The increase in brightness thus continues as it approaches the Earth while at the same time the area of its lit part decreases, until it reaches inferior conjunction (Earth–Venus–Sun) and is at its closest position to the Earth, about 0.28 astronomical units away, equal to 41 million km. After moving from inferior conjunction it is very bright even though it is in the crescent phase, and as we noticed, Venus is one of the bodies that approach the Earth closely.

The Sun rises in the west!

I did not make a mistake in writing the title, for the Sun we are used to on Earth rises in the east and sets in the west. But this is not the case on Venus. Scientists, using radio waves that can penetrate clouds, with the aim of studying the planet's surface, analysed the returned waves and interpreted them based on the Doppler effect, and found that the planet moves clockwise, that is, from east to west, while the other planets, for example Mercury as we saw earlier, move from west to east, counter-clockwise. This indicates that a resident of Venus, if we could imagine life there, and how hard it is to imagine life on Venus, would see the Sun rise in the west and set in the east very slowly because of the planet's retrograde motion. From this scientists concluded that the planet must be upside down, with its north pole at the bottom and its south pole at the top, and its axis of rotation tilted about 177° from the plane perpendicular to the planet's axes at the Sun's equator (the ecliptic).

A year passes every day! I am still talking about the oddities of Venus's orbit. The hard-to-imagine resident of this planet grows a year older with every day that passes. We know that a day results from the planet's rotation once on its axis, and because of the planet's very slow rotation on its axis, indeed it is the slowest planet in rotation, it needs 243 days to complete one rotation on its axis to produce a day, and this period is the longest planetary day of the planets of the solar system, even longer than Venus's year!! Venus needs 225 days to complete one orbit around the Sun. So the planet finishes one orbit around the Sun and the year ends without it having finished a single day of rotation on its axis yet! A planet without a magnetic field! This planet has some strange and unfamiliar characteristics for us compared with the Earth. The planet is upside down, and the Sun rises over it from the west and sets in the east. The day on it is longer than the year because of its extreme slowness in rotating on its axis. When uncrewed spacecraft approached the planet carrying probes to measure its magnetic field strength, scientists were surprised that the planet has no magnetic field in the true sense and that the magnetic force they detected hardly exceeds one thousandth of the strength of the Earth's magnetic field.

Paradise or hell?

As we saw above, Venus is considered Earth's twin and is the bright, shining, sparkling one. When the clouds that envelop the planet's surface were observed, the belief began that there must be life, water, oceans and forests beneath this warm veil of clouds. Some gave free rein to their imagination to picture a comfortable life on this warm planet near the Sun, and likened the planet to paradise with its forests, warm springs and mild weather all year round. These imaginings fired the imagination of writers and artists and became the subject of many paintings and drawings. Because humans at that time, using Earth-based observatories, could not study its surface, everyone elaborated on imagination. And what a surprise, and this word is not strange when we talk about Venus, when Venus was studied up close using uncrewed spacecraft, which began from Russia in a series of Venera spacecraft, the first flight to Venus being Venera 1 on 12/2/1961. It was followed by the American Mariner series, the first flight being Mariner 2 on 27/8/1962, and each was followed by a large number of flights such as Pioneer Venus 1 & 2 in 1978, which worked until 1992. Then came the launch of the Magellan probe, which began transmitting pictures and information about the planet in 1990; then this spacecraft entered the layers of the planet's thick atmosphere in 1994, then struck the planet's surface and ended its journey after transmitting extremely important information. From the results of these flights came the surprise. An unknown planet was found, very hot, very dry, and perhaps the best comparison is an empty frying pan left on the fire for a long time, with piled-up clouds and very high atmospheric pressure. Wherever you go you face the same conditions! There is no difference between a night region or a day region, or between an equatorial region and the polar regions: the air is always stiflingly hot and very dry, and there is no better comparison than the word hell. The greenhouse planet:

Space probes studied Venus's thick atmosphere, which extends to great distances above the planet's surface and exerts a pressure on Venus's surface 90 times greater than the atmospheric pressure at the Earth's surface. To picture it, imagine yourself 900 metres deep in the ocean without protection from the water's pressure. Such is the high pressure at Venus's surface. With the extreme pressure the temperature rises greatly, and the lower we descend from the cloud tops towards the planet's surface the higher the temperatures climb, reaching nearly 750 K (400–460 °C). All of this is because of the very thick layer of clouds surrounding the planet! From studying the high reflectivity of their particles it was found that they are clouds containing droplets of sulphuric acid, and this is the reason for their pale yellow colour. The rain on this planet's surface is acid rain, but these droplets falling towards the planet's ground evaporate again before reaching the surface because of the enormous amounts of heat. The source of these gases, such as sulphur and sulphur dioxide, is the volcanoes on the planet's surface, which emitted them, after which thermal chemical reactions between them took place in the hot cloud layers, together with photochemical reactions that led to the accumulation of acid. This thick atmosphere surrounding Venus is made up mostly of carbon dioxide gas, whose concentration reaches 97%, and nitrogen at 3%, and these gases work as greenhouse gases: when sunlight falls on the planet it penetrates the clouds to reach the surface and is then reflected from the surface, but the gases in the atmosphere absorb part of the reflected infrared radiation and return the remainder to the surface again, so heat is trapped inside. Energy absorption thus continues and the temperature keeps rising, until the temperature on Venus became one of the highest among the members of the solar system! It is not, as some think, that Mercury has the highest temperature because it is the nearest to the Sun. That is because on Venus, on any spot of this planet's ground, whether night or day, polar or equatorial, the temperature is high, so there is no difference between any part at any time in terms of high temperature. Studies of the planet using ultraviolet rays showed storms and violent winds moving at great speed, reaching 350 km/h from east to west in the upper reaches of the atmosphere, so that they circle the planet in four days. The lower we go towards the planet's surface the slower the clouds move, reaching 3–18 km/h at an altitude of 10 km, and at an altitude of 7 km the clouds disappear entirely.

It is worth mentioning that in 1978 a doubling of the sulphuric acid level in Venus's atmosphere was measured, reaching fifty times the level first measured, which led scientists to suggest that an eruption on the planet's surface may have occurred.

Structure of the planet:

The probes sent to the planet were able to tell us about the planet's structure. One of the most important structures is that of the atmosphere. The information received divided its atmosphere into three main layers of sulphur clouds: the upper, outer layer is characterised by many small droplets of sulphuric acid; below it lies the middle layer, characterised by larger and less numerous droplets of sulphuric acid; then a dense lower layer characterised by its larger droplets; below it there is a haze reaching an altitude of 40 km above the planet's surface, and after that, as we approach the surface at an altitude of 30 km, the very dry air and very high heat begin, so nothing can remain without evaporating until we reach the planet's surface, scorching in its high heat, whose internal structure is believed to be very similar to the Earth's.

Surface features:

The maps we have of Venus's surface are radar maps and pictures from spacecraft that landed on it, as the human eye could not see the planet's surface directly. The planet is mostly smooth, and about 10% of it consists of highlands, some reaching about 11 km. Among the most famous continents is the region of Ishtar Terra in the northern part of the planet, with an area equal to that of the continent of Australia (2,900 km across). In this continent there is an elevated plateau called Lakshmi Planum, reaching 1,500 km in width and surrounded by a mountain range of varying height, containing the highest peak, Maxwell Montes, reaching 11 km on the eastern side with a base width of nearly 700 km (it is interesting to mention that this is the only place named after a male, the scientist James Clerk Maxwell; apart from this name, all other places were named after female goddesses and heroines of myth or famous women from history). It also contains a huge crater 100 km across called Cleopatra, which the Magellan mission confirmed, from the pictures it transmitted of it, to be a volcanic crater. The other distinctive highland on the planet's surface is the continent Aphrodite Terra south of the planet's equator, whose area scientists estimate at that of Africa, and which is larger than Ishtar Terra. Its features clearly show eastern highlands and western highlands divided by a depression 3–4 km deep and 1,000 km long, representing a deep valley called Diana Chasma. It was believed the cause of this valley was the movement of this planet's plates in a manner similar to the movement of plates on Earth, but the Magellan mission to the planet showed that there is no phenomenon of plate movement in the planet's crust.

The pictures also showed a number of craters on the planet's surface, some from meteorites, especially those that extend as a chain of craters as if huge meteorites broke up as they fell, and some from volcanoes that erupted on the planet's surface. In the northern part of the planet's surface there is the region Phoebe Regio–Beta Regio, which contains two famous craters, Rhea and Theia. The results of the Magellan mission showed that the surface is covered with lava flows and that the planet is still volcanically active in a number of hot regions. It was observed that the meteorite craters on the planet's surface are large, the largest reaching 160 km in diameter and the smallest ranging from 5 to 8 km, which means small craters are absent. The reason for this is attributed to the fact that smaller material is completely ionised in the thick atmosphere, so nothing of its material remains to reach the planet's surface and form small craters.

Why is the Earth not like Venus?

Scientists believe that at the beginning of the solar system's formation the conditions on Venus and Earth were similar and the Sun was then less hot. When the heat from the Sun intensified, and because Venus is nearer the Sun than the Earth (it is only 108 million km away), the oceans began to evaporate and CO₂ began to be released from chalky rocks by the heat, and this gas began to accumulate in the atmosphere and gradually trapped heat. The temperature began to rise, evaporation increased, the accumulation of CO₂ in the atmosphere increased, and so the greenhouse effect increased. The planet's temperature began to rise. And so on until clouds and gases piled up and the atmosphere became dry and hot. The Sun could not have this effect on the Earth because of its distance from it, which averages 150 million km. Now the question that poses itself: if we took the Earth and placed it in Venus's place, what would happen? First, the amount of sunlight falling on it would double and the planet's temperature would begin to rise, then the water in the oceans would evaporate, and CO₂ in the atmosphere would increase from the chalky surface rocks because of the heat, so its accumulation would increase the greenhouse effect, which raises the atmosphere's temperature, which increases the accumulation of CO₂ in the atmosphere, and so on.. until the atmosphere becomes dry and hot. Once this chain of reactions begins it will never stop. There is no doubt of God's power and wisdom when He placed the Earth at this ideal distance to preserve for humans an ideal atmosphere for life. It is worth mentioning that this planet has no moons.

The Earth

The Earth is the only planet pulsing with life, with features unique among the members of the solar system that allowed life to develop on its surface. It is a dense, solid rocky sphere, indeed one of the densest planets, with an average density of 5.5 g/cm³, and the Earth's mass is 6 × 10²⁴ kg, so the Earth's volume is about 10²⁷ cm³. The Earth is not perfectly spherical: it bulges at the middle, its equatorial radius being 6,378 km while its radius from the poles is 6,357 km. The reason for this difference is the Earth's rotation on its axis, which causes a centrifugal force that pushes material from the equator towards the far side, leading to its equatorial flattening. The Earth rotates on itself, and its axis of rotation is tilted by 23.4° from the perpendicular to the plane of the ecliptic, and it takes nearly 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds to rotate once on itself, a rotation that produces night and day. It takes about 365.25 days to complete one orbit around the Sun, which produces the year. The Earth's average distance from the Sun is the astronomical unit, which equals about 150 million km. The eccentricity of the Earth's orbit is e = 0.017.

The Earth's orbit is an ellipse, according to Kepler's first law. For those who do not know an elliptical orbit, this information is useful: it is not like a circle, which has only one centre, but has two centres (foci), and the Sun lies at one of these two foci. This suitable distance from the Sun gives the Earth the unique distinction of a suitable temperature that allows water to remain in the liquid state, something we will not see on the other planets. A student of the Earth notices that about 71% of the Earth's surface is covered with water, and these bodies of water are called the oceans. The remainder (about 29%) of the surface is a solid, dry part called the continents. The rocks of these continents are older, as the Earth's age is estimated at about 4.54 billion years, while the rocks of the ocean floor are estimated at only about 200 million years old. The rocks of the continents were found to be possibly silicates, formed mainly of the elements oxygen and silicon mixed with other elements and minerals, or they may be basalts with very fine grains. The rocks of the Earth's crust are divided in general into three types of terrestrial rocks: igneous, metamorphic and sedimentary.

An observer of the Earth's surface notices that part of its crust protrudes to form the Earth's most important relief, the mountains, which we may find as mountain ranges, the highest being the summit of Everest at about 8.85 kilometres above sea level. These ranges are not a feature of the continent's rocks only, for we can see mountain ranges on the ocean floors too. An observer of the Earth can distinguish two domes of ice covering the Earth's north and south poles, whose patches extend in winter and shrink in summer. The oceans adjacent to the two icy poles have waters frozen by the intense cold, but because of the anomalous property of water this freezing is only of the top surface layer of the water and the depths do not freeze. Water, unlike other liquids, expands as it cools on freezing and its density decreases, so it rises upwards and only the ocean's surface freezes, allowing other forms of life to stay alive beneath it.

Mars

Mars is one of the planets known since ancient times, and because of its brightness and its red colour, clearly seen in the night sky, it was named after the god of war, Mars. Red is the colour of war and death among the Romans. Mars is the fourth planet in distance from the Sun and the seventh in size. It is the first planet to orbit outside the Earth's orbit, with the properties that follow from this that differ from what we saw when studying Mercury and Venus, such as the possibility of observing this planet for long periods in the night sky. It crosses the sky from east to west. And since its orbit is outer, no transits across the Sun's disc occur for it. But if we suppose a resident on Mars's surface, he could see the Earth crossing in front of the Sun's disc and would see the Earth rise to a certain level in the sky and would not observe it for long in Mars's night. Mars is 228 million km from the Sun (1.52 astronomical units). The length of its orbit around the Sun is longer because the size of the orbit has become large now, and we will take up these topics in the course of this chapter.

The planet of science fiction: this planet has fired the imagination of scientists, writers and artists greatly, and everyone spent a long time trying to address the inhabitants of Mars and imagine their forms, characteristics and the nature of their life. A prize was even offered on 17/12/1900 by the wealthy French widow Clara Guzman, worth 10 thousand francs, for research and communication with any life beyond the Earth other than Mars! Because they were completely sure Mars was inhabited and teeming with life. Stories, tales, novels and various writings about the inhabitants of Mars multiplied, and the process of contacting these inhabitants was active but to no avail. Even at the opposition of Mars on 25/8/1924, when Mars approached the Earth to only 55.78 million km, the US Army responded to a proposal by the astronomer Todd to stop all radio transmission the army sends and listen for radio signals the inhabitants of Mars might send to the Earth. This was repeated at the opposition of 1926 and the opposition of 1928.

Now we must ask what the reason was for all this interest in Mars. Why did we not notice this interest when studying the previous planets? When studying Mars in a preliminary way, scientists noticed two icy domes in the north and south of the planet, like the icy poles on Earth. These two domes change in area over time. If we know that the area of the icy poles on Earth changes with the four seasons, shrinking in summer and increasing in winter, and the reason for the four seasons on Earth is the tilt of the Earth's axis from the perpendicular to its orbit by 23.4° as the Earth moves in its orbit around the Sun, then the reason for the difference in the area of the poles on Mars over the year must indicate that seasons occur on its surface, and these seasons arise only from the tilt of the planet's axis from the line perpendicular to its orbit. The planet's axis must therefore show a tilt from the perpendicular to the orbit, and indeed its axis was found to be tilted, this degree of tilt being 25.2°, a value close to the tilt of the Earth's axis. Many thought that with changing seasons there must be life.

Some observations were made whose results reinforced such an idea, as some observers of the planet's surface were able to see dark patches varying in colour, so they believed them to be oceans and continents like the Earth's. Some white clouds were seen in certain regions of its surface. Moving sandstorms were also observed. When the scientist Christiaan Huygens studied the planet and drew the first map of it, he found that the Martian day is about 24 hours (in fact the correct figure for the Martian day is 24 hours, 37 minutes and 22.6 seconds), very close to the Earth's day. The existence of night and day of the same length as the Earth's night and day was linked in people's thinking at that time to the existence of life similar to life on Earth. And when two small moons were observed orbiting near the planet at the level of the equator, some scientists believed they were artificial satellites orbiting the planet. Such sightings must have stirred the imagination that there must be Martian life, indeed that repeated attempts should be made to contact them.

Studying the planet up close:

Scientists were encouraged to study this planet up close, and that the time had come to fly spacecraft towards it to learn about it. The first fruits of this activity, which succeeded compared with the failed spacecraft launched before it, were the American spacecraft Mariner 4, launched on 28 November 1964 towards Mars, which managed to transmit some important information. Among it: that the planet has a very thin atmosphere made mostly of CO₂ with a very low atmospheric pressure. The probes could not detect any trace of a magnetic field around the planet, and the pictures also showed clear craters of various kinds on its surface. Then the spacecraft Mariner 6 and then 7 were launched in 1969 and gave information similar to their predecessor's. In 1971, specifically on 30 May, Mariner 9 was launched and reached a distance of 1,370 km from the planet's surface. This spacecraft gave us the most important information and the most marvellous pictures, and added a great deal to what we need to know about Mars, and it kept transmitting until 27 October 1972.

But studying the existence of life on Mars still needed probes that land on the planet's surface and carry out the necessary studies, and these ideas were realised by launching the twin spacecraft Viking, two similar spacecraft V-1 and V-2, which landed on the planet's surface, the first on 20 July 1976, the seventh anniversary of the landing of humans on the Moon, its landing site being in the Chryse basin at 22.4° north and 47.5° west. It was followed by the landing of the second spacecraft on 3 September 1976 at the site Utopia, 47.9° north and 225.9° west. How wonderful it was when the two spacecraft began transmitting pictures and information about the planet's surface, the composition of the soil and atmosphere, the surface temperature and tests for the existence of life of any kind… etc. The two spacecraft's location faced the Earth to receive the messages, which took about 19 minutes to reach the Earth, after which the studies on this information would begin immediately. From these studies it became clear that there is no form of life on this planet's surface, even in its simplest forms. Many who had hoped for the existence of neighbours of ours on Mars were disappointed. But the consolation remains that the search was done at only two points on this planet's surface and that the size of the samples was very small, and perhaps we searched at the wrong time and place.

This thin atmosphere is made mostly of CO₂, which reaches 95% of its components. Local storms were observed on the surface, which are strong winds blowing rapidly and forming dust clouds and whirlwinds that spin on the surface and move the soil from place to place. These winds that blow on the planet have, as on Earth, a daily wind cycle and a seasonal cycle. They have a great effect on the processes of erosion and weathering on the planet's surface. And because the atmosphere's density is 2% of the Earth's atmosphere's density, the wind's force must be about 7 to 8 times greater than the force of Earth's winds in order to stir and carry dust and form whirlwinds. Earth winds at a speed of 24 km/h stir these storms, but on Mars we need winds at a speed of 180 km/h to produce such storms. Among the clear evidence of the Aeolian effect of wind movement are sand dunes, where winds carry sand from one place and drop it in another, so we find them clearly extended across the planet's surface. When an amount of dust is stirred up, the storm maintains itself by converting solar energy into kinetic wind energy, absorbing energy from solar radiation, heating the atmosphere and increasing the wind's speed, so a yellowish mantle of whirlwinds wraps the planet. Because there is no water to wash the dust from the atmosphere, it remains suspended for several weeks before settling on the surface again. Strangely, these winds blow quietly and without sounds, so the sounds of Earth's roaring storms do not apply to them.

Water on Mars:

The ideas of scientists studying this planet were linked to the existence of water networks on its surface. Even the preliminary maps they drew included such networks. The prevailing belief was that water flows on the surface of Mars. The scientist Giovanni Schiaparelli presented a map he had been preparing since 1877, taking advantage of the opposition of 1879, and announced it in 1881, astonishing everyone because it shows water channels on Mars's surface, which he called canali, and he put 62 names on it, most of them mythological names and some of them Arab names such as the Litani, the Euphrates and the Nile and others. As for Percival Lowell, a wealthy diplomat who left politics and took up astronomy as a hobby and built an observatory in Arizona to observe Mars, and who was among those who believed in a Martian civilisation, he convinced the general public that the clearly seen channels were irrigation canals supplying the planet with water from the polar regions. All of this is of course illusion! But after uncrewed spacecraft approached the planet and landed on its surface, everyone became sure that there is no liquid water now, but there are signs that cannot be overlooked indicating that water flowed on its surface in bygone times. These forms are river channels and dry gullies that are clearly visible. They indicate that these waters must have eroded the valleys, and they are responsible for these forms resembling river courses, such as their sources and mouths on Earth. These indications began with Mariner's pictures of the planet's surface, but where did this water go now?

The two poles are considered the water reservoir on Mars. But in winter, as we mentioned, carbon dioxide gas in the atmosphere condenses, freezes and falls onto the planet's surface and contributes to increasing the size of the ice dome, reaching 30% of the volume of the hemisphere at each pole. In the Martian summer the size of the domes decreases to 1%. It is believed that the planet's north pole is made of water ice that sublimates into the atmosphere with the summer heat and contributes to the presence of a proportion of water vapour among the components of the atmosphere. But where is the largest part of this water? It must be stored beneath the soil, or the water may be held in accumulated frozen layers like the Earth's south pole, which is called permafrost, that is, an accumulation of frozen layers of water reaching a thickness of about 1 km. Most of the water is expected to exist at the poles in the form of this permafrost or it may be beneath the soil. Where did this water come from? And where did it go? It remains a mystery calling for a search for its solution. With all this talk of water flowing on the planet's surface previously, the planet's surface is now completely dry. There are some surface forms that explain the earlier presence of water, such as the shapes of some strange craters, which indicate that there must have been water ice when they formed in order to explain why their shape is strange. Large-diameter craters differ from those on Mercury and the Moon, because the impacts generated heat that melted the frozen ice in the planet's ground, and this water acted as a lubricant for the resulting debris, which flowed around the crater in waves until it dried and hardened or cooled and froze, and traces of the liquid flow can be seen around the crater.

Images containing forms indicating flood water channels were studied, that is, surges or sudden floods that led to the formation of some features such as tear-drop shaped islands, estimated to be three billion years old, which indicate that the surges were from the southern highlands to the northern lands. The planet needs extensive study to answer many questions, the most important of which is the subject of water on Mars. Its atmosphere is light and its atmospheric pressure low, which confirms there is no liquid water now. Perhaps four billion years ago it had a thick, warm atmosphere that made liquid water possible, then a climate change occurred that froze these flowing rivers. Then for some reason the temperature rose and the ice melted, forming huge surges that led to the formation of some forms on the surface as we described, then Mars became dry and frozen again, and God Almighty knows best what happened.

The poles of Mars:

However much we speak of Mars's poles we always find new subjects to add. As we said earlier, the poles are icy, mostly perhaps carbon dioxide gas, and these poles show differences in their area according to the seasons. The poles consist of two zones, a permanently frozen zone (residual caps) and a zone subject to freezing and melting, which makes the conditions on it variable (seasonal caps). The first occupies a smaller area but is more brilliant, and at the south pole extends to 350 km, and its temperature reaches 150 K (−120 °C), while at the north pole this zone extends to 1,000 km and its temperature exceeds 200 K (−70 °C), and it is believed to consist of water ice, which is the reason for the presence of a greater proportion of water vapour above the north pole in summer. It is therefore likely that the north pole is the water reservoir. But what raises the question now is why the north pole is warmer by about 50°. The reason is believed to be the eruption of storms at the south pole in summer, their period of occurrence extending over a quarter of a Martian year. The dust storms rush from south to north, where they absorb heat by their motion, as we mentioned, and warm the atmosphere, so the temperature rises more in the north, and perhaps this is the reason for the clear presence of sand dunes in Mars's northern hemisphere. The spacecraft Mariner 9 confirmed the existence of such dust storms that shroud the northern part of the planet, and was able to observe only four dark points that stood out above the storm, which we will get to know later

Surface features of Mars:

Explorers of this planet have often talked about its terrain and the nature of its surface, so much so that they imagined one of the pictures sent of the planet's surface to be rock carvings and pyramids, but it was later shown that they were due to erosion and weathering factors on the planet's surface. The first information sent about the planet's surface was by Mariner 9, which transmitted a picture of the planet cloaked in a dust storm that hid all features except four dark points. Later the storm calmed and the picture of the planet's surface began to clear, and it imaged, as we saw earlier, dry riverbeds, valleys, faults, volcanic craters and others. Then the twin Viking spacecraft began transmitting information about the surface, each according to its location, and the outcome of these studies was to distinguish the difference between the planet's northern and southern halves in terms of terrain. The planet's southern half had older, more numerous and higher craters and contains the two deepest basins, Hellas and Argyre. Scientists estimated the age of events on this part at about four billion years. The planet's northern half was level land with fewer volcanic craters and low-lying, recent regions. Scientists estimated the age of its events at three billion years.

Mariner 9 also transmitted a picture of the most famous valleys of Mars, among the largest valleys in the solar system, which was named after the spacecraft Valles Marineris, 4,500 km long, 600 km wide and up to 7 km deep below the surface. This valley is linked to the region of bulges called the Tharsis bulges, which the spacecraft imaged as four dark points. It was later learned that they are summits of highlands exceeding the Earth's Everest by about three times, which is why these summits appeared like dark points standing out above the storm. The reason for this valley's occurrence, as scientists explained, is a tectonic fracture in the planet's crust that changed the terrain and produced this clear feature in the planet's equatorial region. It is one of the largest known valleys on the surface of all the solar system's planets. It cannot be the work of humans or of the courses of rivers; the most accepted reason for the formation of this valley is the fracturing of the Martian crust. As for the four famous bulges, they are volcanic mountains, and the most famous of these volcanic mountains on Mars, distinguished by their very high summits, is Olympus Mons. It is the highest volcanic mountain in the whole solar system, its height estimated at about 25 km above the surrounding levels, its width at the base 600 km, and the diameter of its crater at the summit 80 km. This mountain is considered a very huge volcano compared with the largest terrestrial volcano, Mauna Loa in Hawaii, which is 9 km high from the ocean floor and 120 km wide at the base. After it come three other, slightly smaller and lower summits lying on one line. The flow of lava from them forms a scene similar to Earth's volcanoes. These three summits are: Ascraeus Mons, Pavonis Mons and Arsia Mons

The question here is why were Mars's highlands so huge? Scientists believe the reason is related to the thickness of Mars's crust. It is a small planet, smaller than the Earth, so it cooled quickly and developed a rocky shell thicker than the Earth's shell, which was too strong to fracture into plates. This gave it a longer chance to develop. When volcanoes erupted, the force of their thrust was enough to fracture this thick crust. And because the planet's gravity is low (0.4 of Earth's gravity), the weight of these thrusts decreased and so their thrust and rise upwards increased until they formed these towering summits. Based on this gravity, the height of mountains reaches about 2.5 times the height of Earth's mountains. The Earth, by contrast, is larger in size, so its crust was thin and fractured into plates, characterised by the appearance of small and numerous volcanic mountain chains such as the Hawaiian volcanic chain, a chain of volcanic islands in the Pacific Ocean, and others. Because gravity on Earth is greater, the thrust of lava and its accumulation were small. There has been no proof that Mars's volcanoes are still active, although some scientists believe eruptions still occur in them but have not been imaged by any previous spacecraft. There are also many meteorite craters on the surface of Mars and its two moons. These craters differ clearly when compared with the craters of the Moon and Mercury, because, as we mentioned earlier, the ejecta were fluid in consistency and extended to the sides of the craters before solidifying. Also weathering factors perhaps play their part in changing the shapes of these craters or burying them with the sand dunes spread across this planet's surface, where such erosion factors do not appear on the Moon and Mercury. When the spacecraft Viking landed and sent the first close picture of the planet's surface, it was marvellous to see the pink sky and the planet's pink and red rocks, and it differed clearly from the picture of the Moon, which appeared with dark grey rocks and sky. One of the most important tasks assigned to Viking was analysing the soil. In the area of Viking 1 the planet's surface appeared covered with a layer of limonite, which is hydrated iron oxide in its simplest form (rust), which as we all know is red in colour.

These rocks, in their various forms, were made of the following materials at both sites: iron, calcium, silicon, titanium, aluminium, magnesium, sulphur, potassium, iron oxide and silicon oxide. The soil contained no form of organic molecules, even in samples taken from beneath the rocks, on the thought that these rocks might provide a shield from the ultraviolet rays that destroy forms of life, yet the results were negative at both sites too. From knowing the volume and the mass we can calculate the planet's density, which was found to equal 3.9 g/cm³. The planet has a very weak magnetic field, equal to 0.001 of the Earth's field. The planet's orbit:

Mars, as we presented, is the fourth planet in distance from the Sun. It is the first planet with an orbit outside the Earth's orbit, and it lies about 228 million km from the Sun on average. Its eccentricity is e = 0.093, relatively large, which shows that its orbit is clearly elliptical: at perihelion it is 206 million km from the Sun and on reaching aphelion it is 249 million km from the Sun. We see a clear difference between the two distances, and this leads to a variation in the amount of sunlight falling on its surface of up to 45% between aphelion and perihelion, a difference of 30 °C, with the changes in the planet's climate between the two locations that follow from it. The surface temperature ranges between winter and summer from −144 °C to 27 °C, while on average the temperature is estimated at about −23 ° to −55 °C.

The planet completes this orbit in a time equal to 687 Earth days, and while it moves in this orbit a number of phenomena occur to it. We will get to know some of them in the following lines. Opposition: this phenomenon occurs when the planet is at perihelion and Mars, the Earth and the Sun line up in a straight line, the Earth in the middle. This astronomical phenomenon recurs once every 780 days, and then the distance between Mars and the Earth is at its least, but it is not the same at different oppositions because Mars's orbit around the Sun is flattened and elliptical, not circular as is nearly the case with the Earth's orbit. The distance at opposition therefore ranges between 55 million km when Mars is at perihelion and the Earth at aphelion, and 102 million kilometres with Mars at aphelion and the Earth at perihelion. The last of these oppositions was on 28 August 2003, but the closest distance between the two planets was on 27/8/2003, when the planet's distance reached only about 55,758,006 km from the Earth. The planet's apparent brightness was then about −2.9 and its apparent diameter 25.1 arcseconds, a size very suitable for observing the planet's surface with telescopes. This opposition phenomenon is important to space agencies because they exploit the closeness in distance to launch spacecraft towards the planet to explore it. In general Mars appears in the sky bright with a red colour because its soil contains iron oxides, and it is less brilliant than Venus. The reason is that the reflectivity of its surface, 0.15, is lower than the reflectivity of Venus's surface, 0.7. Mars rotates on its axis, as we knew earlier, in a period equal to 24.6 Earth hours, similar to the Earth's day.

The moons of Mars:

Mars's two moons, Phobos and Deimos, were discovered by the astronomer Asaph Hall at the opposition of 1877. As is characteristic of the inner terrestrial planets, which have few moons, Mars has two moons, but unfortunately its moons are very small and could be considered moonlets. These two moons, Phobos and Deimos, are very small in size and irregular in shape, orbit in the plane of the planet's equator very close to it, are of low density, about 2 g/cm³, and are dark on the surface with many meteorite craters. The moon Phobos was named after one of the horses that pull the chariot of the Greek god of war and means fear. Its dimensions are 27 km in length and 22 km in width, and it is distinguished by a large crater reaching 10 km in diameter called Stickney. It orbits Mars at a distance of 9,378 km from Mars's centre in a circular orbit in which it always turns the same face to Mars. Deimos was named after the second horse that pulls the chariot of the Greek god of war and means terror. This moon is slightly smaller than the previous one, its dimensions 15 km in length and 12 km in width. Its surface contains a large crater reaching 2 km in diameter. This moon orbits at a distance of 23,459 km from the planet's centre and is the outer moon. Also in a circular orbit, always giving the same face to Mars. It orbits the planet every 31 hours.

Scientists believe they are two asteroids that Mars captured when they approached it and became two moons orbiting it.

The asteroids

The asteroid belt is a belt of small bodies lying between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter. The average distance of Mars's orbit is 1.5 astronomical units from the Sun, while the average distance of Jupiter's orbit exceeds five astronomical units.

Scientists wondered what fills this orbit.
There must be a planet in this region that had not yet been discovered. Scientists began searching this location for a planet, but instead they found tens of thousands of small asteroids in this orbit, and this was at the beginning of the nineteenth century, in 1801 specifically. The first of the discoveries was the largest of these asteroids, Ceres, about 1,000 km in diameter, by the Italian astronomer Piazzi, and after that date discoveries of these asteroids followed one after another until their number exceeded hundreds of thousands of asteroids. Because of their small size they were called asteroids, that is, little planets.

The shape of these asteroids resembles potatoes, irregular in shape, and their rocky surfaces are full of meteorite craters. It is worth mentioning that the asteroid Ceres was added to the list of dwarf planets after the decision of the International Astronomical Union at its meeting in the city of Prague in August 2006. Scientists believe the origin of the asteroids is a planet that failed to coalesce and form because it lay between the two giant gravitational forces in the solar system, Jupiter's gravity on one side and the Sun's gravity on the other.

When these rocky and metallic pieces were studied it was found that their origins go back to the age of the solar system for the most part, which means they formed with the formation of the solar system and its planets 4.6 billion years ago, and when the masses of these bodies were gathered it was found that they form a body 1,500 kilometres across, that is, less than half the diameter of our Earth's Moon. The size of these asteroids varies greatly: the largest is the asteroid Ceres, and the smallest does not exceed the size of small pebbles.
Asteroids are given a serial number when discovered, and then afterwards they are named: for example Ceres bears the number 1, the asteroid Pallas bears the number 2, and so on. These pieces were larger, and with their motion and colliding with each other they broke into smaller pieces. Some of these asteroids may have moons orbiting them, such as the asteroid Ida.

These asteroids rotate on themselves in several hours, and around the Sun in an elliptical orbit every 3–6 years, and asteroids consist mainly of rock and are rich in metals such as iron and others. Scientists are interested in studying asteroids because they still represent the first formation of the solar system, so studying them is as if we were studying the ancient history of the planets. It is believed that Mars's moons Phobos and Deimos are among the asteroids that were captured by the planet's gravity to take up an orbit around it.

Jupiter

When our talk reaches the fifth planet in distance from the Sun you truly feel the greatness of this planet, so that it is said Jupiter bought grandeur for itself. It is the giant among the planets, bright and brilliant, it and its large moons which can be seen with the smallest telescopes. Only Venus precedes it in brightness. Its brilliance comes from the hugeness of its size, for it is the king of the planets and the largest in size. If we take the Earth's diameter as one, its diameter is 11 times the Earth's diameter, and so its volume is more than 1,338 times the Earth's volume. Imagine that if we assumed it to be a hollow sphere we would need nearly 1,338 Earth-spheres to fill the planet's cavity from the inside. And its mass is about 318 times the Earth's mass. Scientists believe Jupiter's mass alone is more than twice the masses of the rest of the planets combined. From these figures we feel that the Earth is no more than a small point beside this big giant. In this chapter we will study more information about this planet.

Jupiter in numbers

Jupiter goes round the Sun in its orbit nearly upright, as its axis is tilted only 3° from the perpendicular to the plane of its orbit. It orbits at an average distance of 778 million km from the Sun and its eccentricity is e = 0.048. The planet's radius is about 71,400 km. It is not spherical but tends to flatten at the equator, as it appears bulged at the middle, and the figures show this: its radius at the equator equals 71,400 km while its polar radius equals 66,800 km. This is attributed to its fast rotation on itself. Jupiter's mass is 1.9 × 10²⁷ kg, very small compared with the hugeness of its volume, and from these figures the density is calculated and comes to 1.3 g/cm³, so the planet is light. It was found to consist of gas in its entirety. Scientists say that if the planet were entirely gaseous, that is, its core also gaseous, its flattening (bulging) at the middle would be much greater. But from the proportion of its flattening at the middle it must have a small rocky core, expected to equal 10–20 times the Earth's mass. This giant orbits the Sun at a distance of 778 million km. Its orbit is therefore long and so its year will be long, about 11.89 Earth years. That is, it needs about 12 Earth years to complete one orbit around the Sun. But it rotates on itself at an enormous speed, forming the shortest day of all the planets' days, as the day on it is 9 hours, 55 minutes and 29 Earth seconds. Night and day pass over the planet's surface in only 10 hours. And because it is nearly upright it has no four seasons as is the case on Earth.

Jupiter through the telescope

The first to point a telescope at Jupiter was the Italian scientist Galileo Galilei, as he observed the planet and saw four small moons orbiting it. He thereby became certain, confirming Copernicus's theory, that the Earth is not the centre of the universe, and that it is not necessarily the case that all celestial bodies orbit it. Here are Jupiter's four moons orbiting it as if a miniature system, so the Earth is not a centre. These four famous moons have names that are said to have been given them by Simon Marius in 1610, and they are: Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto. They are called the Galilean moons. As we mentioned earlier, one can verify the truth of this by looking through a small telescope towards Jupiter, and we will be able to see its bright disc and its four moons orbiting it. But we may find one of them hidden behind the planet or passing in front of it so that we do not see it, but if we wait a while it will reappear, because they are fast in their orbits around the planet. We will study these moons in detail later. And if we look carefully at Jupiter's disc, or use a larger telescope, the disc will appear more clearly. We will see the planet's disc covered with coloured belts, or at least varying in their colours between dark and light, in latitudinal lines parallel to the planet's equator. To distinguish between these regions scientists call the lit or bright part a zone, while the opaque or dark part is called a belt.

Scientists then divided the planet into three sections comprising a group of zones or belts, which are:
* The northern section, comprising a north temperate zone, a north temperate belt and a north tropical zone.
* The equatorial section, comprising a north equatorial belt, an equatorial zone and a south equatorial belt.
* The southern section, comprising a south tropical zone, a south temperate belt and a south temperate zone.
One can notice a large red spot in the south tropical zone. This spot, known for more than 300 years, was first seen by the English astronomer Robert Hooke in the mid-seventeenth century, specifically in 1664–1665, and is called the Great Red Spot. It moves around the planet in a complete and constant cycle ever since we have known it. The prevailing belief was that it is a solid body floating above Jupiter's gases. A number of oval spots like the red spot can be seen, but smaller in size and different in colour, and I will later explain these phenomena.

Jupiter through spacecraft cameras

The phenomena on Jupiter's surface increased scientists' enthusiasm to visit this planet and observe it up close through the eyes of uncrewed spacecraft that add to us much of what we do not know about it. Topping the list was the flight of the twin spacecraft Pioneer. It comprises two similar spacecraft, Pioneer 10 and Pioneer 11, the first launched on 2 March 1972 from Cape Canaveral and reaching Jupiter in December 1973. The second was launched in April 1973 and arrived in December 1974. It was followed by the twin spacecraft project Voyager, which also comprises two similar spacecraft, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, launched in 1977 and arriving in succession in March and July 1979. The question that will surely leap to mind now is why the duplication in these spacecraft. Why were two spacecraft launched in each project? This is for several reasons, among which I mention that sameness of programme, devices and tools used saves much money and effort through unity of design and manufacture. Also, if a failure occurs in launching the first spacecraft, the second spacecraft is ready to launch instead of cancelling the project and its failing, and even if a particular fault or breakdown of one of the instruments occurs during the journey, the second spacecraft will achieve the same aim. Perhaps the road towards the giant planet, which is very fraught with dangers, calls for a back-up spacecraft in case the other is destroyed. Pioneer 10 then headed out into space, and Pioneer 11 headed to Saturn and then into space in a direction completely different from the first. As for Voyager 1, it headed to Saturn then into space, while Voyager 2 continued its journey to Saturn, then Uranus, then Neptune, and then left beyond the limits of the planets into outer space. After these flights the spacecraft Galileo was launched towards Jupiter in October 1989. Then the probe Ulysses passed Jupiter in February 1992 to carry out further studies of it.

Jupiter's atmosphere

Jupiter, as we mentioned, is a gas planet of low density, and what we see of the planet was found to be gases accumulated in layers on top of each other, and everything we will list about Jupiter's composition is expectations from the data and information transmitted by the spacecraft about the planet. This gas consists mainly of hydrogen, which forms 89% of its composition, and the second main gas is helium, which makes up 11% of the planet's atmosphere, and what remains is very small amounts of methane and ammonia. In this gaseous atmosphere enormous raging storms and giant hurricanes appeared, sweeping through Jupiter's atmosphere, turning and turning on it in constant motion and forming very clear features, which are none other than the latitudinal belts we see on the planet's surface when observing it: streams of gases moving fast towards the east or towards the west, but mostly towards the east, reaching speeds of 540 km/h. If the currents are rising the colours are light, and if they are descending gas currents the colours are dark, based on the assumption of one scientist, and because the original gas components are not coloured, continuous chemical reactions must occur which, with the fast motion of the gases, lead to these colours: yellow, light blue, brown and red. There is another prevailing belief that says the gases are heated by the planet's internal heat and rise upwards, cool and form clouds of ammonia crystals in the hydrogen gas layer. The clouds form the light zones when they are colder and at a higher altitude than the dark, warm and lower regions, which are expected to be composed of ammonium hydrosulphide.

Among the clear atmospheric phenomena on Jupiter's surface is the Great Red Spot, which the spacecraft showed to be a very huge cyclonic phenomenon rotating around the planet in a counter-clockwise direction over a period of up to 6 days, with a size equal to the diameter of about two Earths. There are also a number of other, smaller spots, and scientists believe the staying power of these spots depends on their size. And because there is no dry land, these storms find nothing standing in their way and so they go on and on. Scientists also expect Jupiter's atmosphere to be composed of three distinct layers: the uppermost and least warm, composed of ammonia gas that freezes in the cold and gives the light colour we see when observing the planet. The second layer, where ammonia combines with hydrogen sulphide and forms brown crystals that have a smell like rotten eggs. These crystals of hydrogen sulphide and ammonia form the dark and hotter colours. As for the third and deepest layer, which no one has been able to see, it may be made of bluish crystals of water ice.

A planet radiating heat

As we knew earlier, planets are cold and do not shine by themselves, and they derive their light and energy from the Sun. Jupiter, like the other planets, has solar radiation falling on it, and because it is in an orbit very far from the Sun, the amount of heat reaching it is only 4% of what reaches the Earth (according to the inverse-square law). But scientists were surprised when studying Jupiter by measuring the analysis of the infrared radiation the planet emits, as it turned out that the planet is thermally incandescent, an incandescent globe, that is, it radiates heat from an internal source. And that the amount of this radiation is (1.7) times the value of what reaches it from the Sun's rays, and that the lower we go in the cloud layers the higher the temperature rises! The only explanation scientists give is that this heat may be latent inside the planet, what remains of the heat of the planet's formation during its birth 4.6 billion years ago, and because of the density of its atmosphere it has cooled very slowly and still radiates this heat to this day.

The planet's internal structure

Because the planet has no rocky surface, no spacecraft has been able to land on its surface and study it to learn its true internal structure. But with the help of information analysed and inferred from successive studies of the planet, scientists were able to propose a model of the planet's internal structure that matches this information. We have learned that heat and pressure rise in value when descending from the level of the outer clouds towards the planet's centre. As we descend towards the centre the atmosphere steadily increases in density from the enormous pressure of the accumulated atmospheric layers, until hydrogen gas turns into liquid hydrogen at a depth starting from a thousand to several thousand km. At a depth of about 20,000 km the pressure values are expected to be 3 million times the Earth's atmospheric pressure (3 million millibar), and under such pressure conditions the hot liquid hydrogen turns into metallic hydrogen, where the molecules are tightly compressed close to one another and the electrons swim freely around the nuclei and form an excellent source of electric current. Beneath this layer of metallic hydrogen lies a small core about the size of two Earths, about 20,000 km across, but with a mass equal to the mass of 15 Earths. The pressure reaches about 500 million times the Earth's atmospheric pressure and the temperature about 40,000 kelvin.

The planet's magnetic field

When the twin Pioneers were launched they revealed belts similar to the two Van Allen belts around the Earth, but these belts extend over much wider distances and range. Later, with studies of the planet, it was discovered that the planet has a strong magnetic field tilted by 11° from the planet's axis of rotation, and that it has two magnetic poles, north and south, but an Earth compass, if we could imagine placing it on the planet, would point towards the planet's geographic south. This field is about 12 times stronger than the Earth's magnetic field and is indeed the strongest magnetic field among all the planets. In this magnetic field electric charges appear representing the particles the planet captured by its magnetic force, and the shape of Jupiter's magnetic field differs, as it elongates outwards as a long tail because of the pressure of the solar wind. This extension reaches billions of kilometres, so that its effect reaches the orbit of Saturn, extending a distance ranging between 50 and 100 times the planet's diameter. Radio waves have been observed from the planet due to the acceleration of particles moving in the magnetic field.

A ring around the planet:

From our study of the planets that come after Mars, which are described as light gas giants with many moons, we will notice a new phenomenon they also share: the presence of rings orbiting around their equatorial middle. Although Saturn's rings are the most famous, the spacecraft Voyager proved the existence of rings around all the gas giant planets, so this became a new common characteristic among them. We will now speak of Jupiter's faint ring, which we have not been and are not able to see from Earth. When the spacecraft Voyager 1 passed near Jupiter in March 1979 a narrow belt was discovered in the plane of the planet's equator. The bright part of this ring was close to the planet's surface and lies at a distance of 1.8 times its central diameter. Jupiter's ring is thin and of little thickness. This ring has been divided into three sections: the one nearest the planet, lying from 100,000 to 122,800 km from the planet's centre with a width of up to 22,800 km and a thin thickness, called the Halo. The second section extends from the end of the first section from 122,800 to 129,200 km with a width of 6,400 km and is called the Main. The third section extends to 214,200 km and is called the Gossamer. The middle part of the ring is the bright one and the remainder is a faint part in which small particles are scattered up to a height of 5,000 km above and below the middle part. This ring looks unclear when observed, as observers could not notice it from Earth because it lies close to the bright planet, whose brilliance overwhelms it so that we do not see it. But after its discovery, using infrared observatories in which the planet appears dim, we can see the bright rings and distinguish them from Earth.

The moon Io

When looking at a picture of this moon you marvel at its wonderful reddish-orange colour with yellow and brown streaks, for it is truly full of colours. As you know from the previous table, it is one of the densest moons and the nearest to the parent planet. It is somewhat similar to our Earth's Moon in size and density, but it is free of meteorite craters, and instead there are volcanic craters, volcanic cones and molten lava thrusts on it, and these thrusts bury and cover any trace on the surface. It is one of the most geologically active moons, after humans had believed that the solar system was inactive and that only the Earth was active. We were surprised by astonishing pictures of very huge volcanic eruptions, so that Voyager 1's camera recorded eight successive volcanic eruptions that surged rapidly upwards, even forming a protrusion at the moon's limb. These eruptions are in the form of an enormous fountain spreading over an area up to 1,400 km across, because of the moon's weak gravity and the absence of an atmosphere. These eruptions are believed to be of sulphur and sulphur dioxide, and the liquid medium of this lava is expected to be molten sulphur dioxide, which meets the very hot molten red sulphur, so the materials surge forcefully to the surface erupting in the form of an enormous fountain. The source of energy in this moon was unknown. If the moon were in a circular orbit facing Jupiter with the same face always, the tidal forces arising from Jupiter's pull on it would not change and there would be no internal energy for the moon. But by studying the moon's orbit it was found that, because of the influence of the other Galilean moons, they cause a pull that draws the moon out of its orbit to and fro, as if an oscillating motion forwards and backwards. These oscillations move materials in the moon's interior, and by this motion and friction the materials melt and generate energy. It can be likened to when you try to cut a wire by moving it back and forth at a certain point: you feel heat there. This explanation is believed to be reasonable and acceptable for the source of heat in the moon's interior. Some hot regions were found, called hot spots, reaching 17 °C while the temperature of neighbouring non-hot regions is −146 °C, which shows there is energy inside it driving the molten lava that gives the moon its distinctive colour. Some scientists believe the source of particles in the magnetic halo surrounding the planet is Io's erupting volcanoes. The moon is affected by the magnetic force of the magnetic field, so these small particles thrown out by the volcanoes gather together and are accelerated to high speeds, orbiting in an orbit called the Io plasma torus, in a shape resembling a round tea doughnut. It contains heavy ions of enormous energy that travel following the moon in its orbit around Jupiter. Among the special strange phenomena of the moon is the presence of a huge halo of sodium ions surrounding the moon, reaching a very large size and moving with the planet during its rotation. They were able to capture it using the Silicon Imaging Photometer System technique

The moon Europa:

The smallest of the four moons in size but the highest in light reflectivity from its surface, so it appears the brightest and most beautiful, distinguished by a very smooth, even surface, so that it is considered unique among all bodies of the solar system for the smoothness of its surface, free of relief. All that can be seen is long dark lines crossing like a net. The moon's surface is believed to be a large frozen water ocean, which is the reason for its high brilliance, and a liquid ocean is expected to exist beneath this frozen crust. When Voyager 2 passed near it at a distance of 204 thousand km, it imaged only three craters not exceeding 20 km in diameter.

The moon Ganymede:

The largest moon in the solar system by far. Its surface is rich in relief and divides into two main regions: a dark region containing a larger number of craters, the most famous of which is the region Galileo Regio, up to 4 thousand km across, believed to be older than the other regions. The other region is light and bright, containing fewer craters but with protrusions and trenches, and is believed to be of more recent age. Because of the moon's density it is expected to be made of ice and rock in similar proportions, with a frozen surface.

The moon Callisto:

A moon full of craters, so much so that it is considered number one in the number of craters populating its surface, and it is dark and of low reflectivity. It is believed to be the oldest among the bodies of the solar system. It contains a large crater 600 km across called Valhalla, surrounded by concentric rings that gradually expand until they reach a ring up to 3 thousand km in diameter.

The big event:

It is not enough for Jupiter to have all the grandeur that distinguishes it as king of the planets in the solar system: it is unique in being the site of the greatest event of our lives! For the first time in human history we saw with our own eyes a real collision of a comet with a planet. What is the story of this comet? What is the story of the collision? What are its consequences? Eugene Shoemaker and his wife Carolyn Shoemaker, who was his unpaid assistant, and their colleague David Levy were working hard photographing and studying near-Earth objects when Carolyn analysed one of the films and, to her astonishment, saw a shattered body around Jupiter! The three could not believe what they were seeing and contacted the Kitt Peak observatory at once to confirm what they had seen. Indeed the sighting of a shattered comet near Jupiter was announced on 25/3/1993. The comet's appearance was very exciting: it looked like a string of pearls with 17 beads lined up on a thread, and this comet was named Shoemaker–Levy 9, abbreviated S-L9, the two names referring to its discoverers and the number 9 indicating the number of periodic comets the team had discovered.

Here scenarios began to find out what had happened to this comet, and among these scenarios, prepared by Yeomans & Chodas, was that the comet orbited Jupiter in 1970 and that in 1992 it came very close to the planet, specifically on 7 July 1992, where it was torn into fragments and began to orbit in an anomalous orbit of eccentricity (e = 0.99), reaching aphelion on 16 July 1993 at a distance of 50 million km from the planet, and the results showed that on its way back the comet would collide with the planet. Here was the excitement everyone awaited impatiently. The comet's path became clear and from it it was determined that the expected impacts would be in the planet's southern hemisphere, specifically at latitude (−45), on 16–22 July 1994, over several days. In this way July shines with a new historic event added to the previous events: on 20–21 July 1969 humans landed on the Moon, and on 20 July 1976 the Viking spacecraft landed on Mars.

This comet, whose diameter is estimated by scientists from Hubble Space Telescope pictures of the fragments at about 10 km, and all of whose fragments shine at fainter than magnitude 20, so its original size cannot have been larger than this, and the largest of these fragments does not exceed about 4 km in diameter. This comet will cause an impact we have not witnessed before. This raises many questions: what will happen? The comet's fragments were 11, then became 17, then became 22. We notice that some fragments evaporated and disappeared and that some large fragments split. The last count of these fragments was 21 fragments, given letters of the alphabet in order, the nearest bearing the letter A and the farthest the letter W, and also given numbers, the farthest being number 1 and the nearest number 21. The nearest was predicted to impact on 16 July and the farthest on 22 July. They are, in order:
A-B-C-D-E-F-G-H-J-K-L-M-N-P2-P1-Q2-Q1-R-S-T-U-V-
From it we notice that fragments O and I disintegrated into dust. As the comet approached, fragments J, M and P1 also disintegrated. We notice that the major fragment Q, on which hope was pinned for a huge explosion, disintegrated into two fragments Q1 and Q2. Likewise fragment P also disintegrated into two fragments. These fragments were accelerating as they approached Jupiter: on 12 July the speed was 7 km/s, increasing to 60 km/s at impact. Scientists expected an enormous release of energy from the impact: if the body struck at a speed of 60 km/s a solid nucleus 1 km across, it would generate an enormous amount of energy equal to a quarter of a million megatonnes of TNT, and if the fragment's diameter became 5 km the energy value would rise by a factor of 600 or more. As one scientist said: had the collision been on the side facing the Earth, we would have seen Jupiter's brilliance even in daytime. But the collision would occur on the dark side away from the Earth, though because of Jupiter's rotation we would see the effect of the impact quickly. For that reason the spacecraft Galileo is the only one directly facing the event and would send results of pictures and analyses in the period after the impact. At the time of the impact the planet was two degrees north-west of the bright fifth-magnitude star Lambda Virginis. The light reflected from its surface needs about 42 minutes and 38 seconds to reach us.

We, the members of the Jordanian Astronomical Society, in our sixth camp, went to the Society's camp site in the Azraq area, in Jordan's eastern desert, to observe this event. Although there was no hope of seeing anything, we had to look at Jupiter in case something unexpected happened. We were not the only ones: in those days all the major and minor observatories and all astronomy amateurs in the whole world pointed their telescopes or binoculars, or even looked with their naked eyes, towards Jupiter, for this was a unique event and nothing so rare had happened since the comet collided 65 million years ago with the Earth and wiped out the dinosaurs and three quarters of life forms on Earth. We could not miss the chance to watch such a unique event. Indeed we watched and waited impatiently, and I still remember how we shouted, jumped and got excited when we saw a strange dark spot on Jupiter's surface that had not been there before but occurred after the collision. But what had happened? We did not know and waited until reports began to reach us. Saturday 16 July was the first collision and no results appeared that night. The next night the sighting of a dark spot at the impact place of fragments A and C was announced, and the enthusiasm for observing and following increased. On Monday 18/7 Jupiter looked as if someone had fired bullets at it from a cannon. The dark spots were very clear, so that they were seen with small telescopes, the most famous being the spot resulting from fragments G and L. The impacts occurred in the upper clouds of Jupiter and did not penetrate deeply. Fireballs formed that rose 2,500–3,000 km upwards. Fragment A was the first fragment to impact and formed a fireball that rose 1,000 km above the surface. Fragment G was the largest of the fragments, with an estimated diameter of 3–4 km. It led to the formation of a fireball, resulting from the force of the explosion, that rose 3,000 km above the surface and a large dark spot estimated to be three times the Earth's diameter. From analysing the components of this fireball ammonia, sulphur and hydrogen sulphide were found. From later analyses new compounds were found such as carbon monoxide, carbonyl sulphide and carbon disulphide. For the impact of fragment H observers were unanimous that a very sharp brilliance and flash appeared on the edge of the planet's circular disc, which indicates a strong explosion with a burst of light. The fall locations of a number of fragments were close to each other because of the planet's fast rotation. Fragment F fell very close to E because it came 9.4 hours later and the planet rotates on itself in about 10 hours, so the fall was close, in the same place. Likewise fragments S and D near fragment G. Fragment W fell at the location of K, and fragment C at the location of A. The angle of inclination of the falling fragments was 45°. They formed various shapes and formed dark spots that extended in a chain. This chain of spots on one line, whose cause is now known, was the key to solving the puzzle of strange chains of meteorite craters lined up on the same line on both moons Ganymede and Callisto. A shattered comet must have collided in succession, forming this series of consecutive craters, which is known as a crater chain (catena), Gipul Catena. The impacts did not affect the planet's orbit. They did not affect its rotation on itself, did not affect its red spot, did not penetrate deeply into it, and were not the spark that would set off nuclear reactions on it.

Saturn

To its observer the planet appears banded latitudinally, parallel to the equator, with coloured lines. But they are not as contrasting as Jupiter's lines, and are dominated by yellow and pale brown. This outer layer consists of frozen ammonia crystals, which is the reason for its yellowish colour. Fierce winds blow on these clouds eastward with the planet's rotation, reaching a speed of 1,800 km/h at the equator. But a reverse motion of the winds occurs at the poles. No huge cyclones like those on Jupiter have been observed, though white oval spots on the planet's surface have been observed, such as those observed in September 1990 by an amateur astronomer, and when the phenomenon was imaged by the Hubble Space Telescope it had turned into a line or belt circling the planet south of the equator.

According to the results of the spacecraft Pioneer and Voyager the planet's gases are composed as in the adjacent table, so that the upper cloud layers are ammonia ice, then ammonium hydrosulphide and hydrogen sulphide, then a layer of helium and gaseous hydrogen, followed by a layer of metallic hydrogen. The planet is believed to have a solid icy rocky core in which some materials such as silica and metals are concentrated, 1,500 km across.

Saturn's magnetic field:

Although the planet's rotation speed on itself is similar to that of Jupiter and the day on both is about 10 hours, Saturn's magnetic field equals 1/20 of the strength of Jupiter's magnetic field. But it is still a thousand times greater than the strength of the Earth's magnetic field.
Saturn's magnetic field is also distinguished by having north and south poles, but opposite to the Earth in their positions, so we see an Earth compass pointing towards Saturn's geographic south to indicate the location of the magnetic north pole on it. This field is exactly aligned with the planet's axis of rotation, whereas on Earth it is tilted by about 10° from the Earth's axis of rotation. It has a regular magnetosphere and an ionised layer of hydrogen atoms around the planet. Scientists determined that the reason for this field's weakness compared with Jupiter's lies in the lesser thickness of its liquid metallic hydrogen layer compared with that on Jupiter.

A planet that emits heat:

Although the heat reaching Saturn from the Sun equals 1% of what reaches the Earth, infrared images showed that the planet radiates heat, which makes it brilliant and bright, and the energy emitted from it equals 1.76 times more than it receives from the Sun, and the temperature of the clouds is 97 K. But Saturn is smaller in size than Jupiter, so it has cooled faster, and this energy it radiates cannot be the heat stored in it during its formation 4.6 billion years ago. There must therefore be another source of this energy. We know that materials dissolve more easily in hot liquids than in cold ones. Also, with a rise in temperature and an increase in pressure it becomes easy for helium to dissolve in metallic hydrogen, just as is expected in Jupiter.

But Saturn is colder and lower in temperature than Jupiter, so we do not expect complete dissolution of helium in metallic hydrogen here. So what happened? Scientists expected that Saturn has lower temperature and pressure and so helium condenses as droplets in the hydrogen and does not dissolve in it. Then, by gravity, they begin to fall downwards towards the planet's centre as if helium rain, and during its movement towards the centre, and by gravity, it is compressed and heated and its energy turns into heat radiated by the planet's interior, just as happens when rain falls on the Earth's surface: its kinetic energy, increased by gravity, turns into heat, and this is what happens on Saturn. Scientists expect that when the helium rain ends the planet will cool and no further heat will return to it other than that derived from the Sun. The concentration of helium is therefore lower in Saturn's gaseous envelope than Jupiter's, and indeed Voyager's observations indicated a helium proportion of only 7%.

1- The moon Phoebe:
It is the farthest, orbiting at a distance of about 13 million km, discovered by Pickering in 1898. This moon is distinguished by a retrograde motion unlike Saturn's other moons, which orbit with the direction of the planet's rotation. It is dark in its makeup and of low reflectivity (0.05), which gives the impression that it is an asteroid that fell into Saturn's capture and began to orbit it.

It orbits at a distance of about 3.5 million km, discovered by Cassini in 1671, and is a strange, wonderful moon called the two-faced one. And because it always keeps the same face towards the planet Saturn as it orbits, we see it bright and then dark when observed from Earth.

The moon Titan:

The largest of Saturn's moons by far, its diameter 5,150 km, slightly smaller than Ganymede, the largest moon of the solar system, orbiting at a distance of 1.2 million km from the planet. Discovered by Huygens in 1655. In 1903 the Spanish scientist Comas Solà predicted an atmosphere on it. In 1944 Gerard Kuiper analysed the planet's spectrum and discovered methane on it, and in 1980 by means of the spacecraft Voyager 1 the presence of nitrogen on it was discovered. It had been planned that the spacecraft would pass close to the moon to study it, and thereby lose its ability to benefit from Saturn's gravity and set off towards Uranus and Neptune, but the benefit of studying this moon would be great, especially with the spacecraft Voyager 2 present to complete the journey to the other planets while Voyager 1 set off into space above the line of the ecliptic. But very unfortunately no features of the moon, bright with a wonderful orange colour, appeared, because of the smog that completely wraps the moon's surface, where photochemical reactions occur in which methane is broken down by ultraviolet rays and reacts with nitrogen, and because of the cold and dryness the smog accumulates. This moon's density is 1.9 g/cm³ and it is therefore expected to have a rocky core forming 55% of the moon's volume surrounded by an icy crust.

Its atmosphere is thick and dense, made up of 90% nitrogen with the remainder methane gas, and the presence of other elements is very small. The atmospheric pressure on it is 60% greater than the Earth's atmospheric pressure at sea level, and scientists therefore expected it to contain about 10 times more gas than the Earth. This moon's temperature is 94 K = −179 °C, so it is frozen. This moon raised many questions because it has an atmosphere while the planet Mercury has none. This atmosphere is made mostly of nitrogen, like the Earth, whose envelope is also mostly nitrogen. But this moon contains no oxygen at all, and its composition is completed by methane gas, which interests us greatly because at this temperature it exists in its three states: as clouds in the atmosphere, liquid in the ocean that is expected to cover the moon's surface, and solid as methane ice. The big question that poses itself: why does Titan contain an atmosphere while the planet Mercury and the moon Ganymede, the largest among the moons, do not?

The two factors that determine the presence or absence of an atmosphere are mass and temperature, and since these three bodies are close in their sizes and masses, the difference between them is due to the second factor, temperature. Mercury, very close to the Sun, has a temperature high enough to evaporate the heaviest gases from its surface, and it cannot keep an atmosphere in any case. As for Ganymede, Jupiter's largest moon, both moons lie far from the Sun but the temperature factor is still the only difference between them. When the two moons were born, Titan's position beside cold, frozen Saturn was enough for water ice to form which absorbed methane and ammonia and froze them in it, compared with Ganymede's warmer position, nearer the giant and hotter, so the situation developed no further than water ice. When Titan's core warmed, it allowed the methane and ammonia to turn from ice to gas, where the Sun's rays broke the ammonia down into hydrogen that escaped into space.

Uranus

The planet Uranus is the third of the giant planets and the seventh of the known planets in distance from the Sun, orbiting at an average distance of 2,900 million kilometres, about 19.2 astronomical units. The eccentricity of its orbit is 0.046, so that at perihelion it is at a distance of 18.3 astronomical units, equal to 2.7 billion km, and at aphelion at a distance of 20.1 astronomical units, equal to 3 billion km. The planet Uranus needs about 84 years to complete one orbit around the Sun, and so it has gone round the Sun only two and a half times since its discovery more than 200 years ago.

This planet orbits the Sun in a strange way, unique among the other planets, as it orbits with its axis tilted by 98°, that is, its axis of rotation on itself nearly coincides with its orbit around the Sun. Thus, as it goes round the Sun it faces it with one of its poles, the north and then the south in turn, and so on. When the spacecraft Voyager 2 reached the planet on 24/1/1986 the south pole was facing the Sun and the Earth, so that the Sun's heat reaches it for 42 years, after which the pole moves into pitch darkness. From here we see that the Sun reaches the poles more than the equator, which is in permanent twilight. Strangely, when temperatures were measured they were found to be nearly the same all over the planet. Knowing that the planet has no internal source of heat, the matter of the closeness of surface temperatures, despite their coldness, still puzzles scientists.

The surface temperature is about 60 kelvin, nearly −213 °C. Some scientists believe that the movement of winds from east to west on its surface at speeds ranging between 200 and 500 km/h is the reason for transferring heat from the lit pole facing the Sun to the dark, cold pole away from the Sun. Or perhaps it is the effect of the heat of the long summer, as the pole had not yet lost its heat when it was on the other, far side when Voyager 2 passed near it, and this is the most accepted reason. Voyager recorded a small number of atmospheric phenomena and motions when it passed near the planet compared with the earlier giants, such as the presence of belts of clouds centred over the pole that was facing the Sun when it passed.

Its magnetic field:

One of the biggest surprises we learned about the planet was its strange magnetic field. Its magnetic pole is tilted about 59° from its axis of rotation on itself. It was also found that this magnetic pole is not centred in the middle of the planet as is the case with the other planets that have a magnetic field, but was found to be about a third of the distance from the planet's edge, that is, it lies on the side of the planet. This situation contradicts the theory that the direction of the planet's magnetic poles roughly coincides with its axis of rotation on itself. Scientists believe that a body collided with the planet Uranus during its formation and caused its axis to tilt 98° from its orbit, and at the same time caused the displacement of the axis of its magnetic poles, but this latter opinion is no longer accepted, as we will see during our study of the planet Neptune. The moment of this magnetic field is about 50 times the moment of the Earth's magnetic field, and a long tail of charged particles extends behind the planet for great distances reaching about 6 million km behind the dark pole. The magnetic field extends to 590 thousand km on the side of the planet's lit pole.
Through studies of emissions and electron storms and calculating their cycle it was learned that the rotation period of the magnetic field equals 17.2 hours, and this is the estimate of the period of the planet's rotation on itself, as scientists could not measure the length of the day on this planet using the phenomenon of changing surface features, as readings ranged between 14 and 17 hours because of the planet's strange motion in its orbit.

The rings of the planet Uranus:

It seems that the presence of rings has become a main characteristic of the giant planets. The planet Uranus contains its own ring system, but they are distinguished from Saturn's rings in being narrow, thin, dark and of low reflectivity, reflecting only 3.2% of the rays falling on them, which makes them dark as coal and hard to see from Earth even with the best telescopes. The history of the discovery of these rings goes back to 10/3/1977, when a team of scientists headed by James Elliot studied the occultation of the star SAO-158687 behind the planet Uranus. Because of imprecision in determining the occultation time, it was decided to begin observing and recording about ¾ of an hour earlier. During this period the star's light dimmed several times, specifically five times, and the reason was attributed to clouds in the sky, but when the dimming recurred after the star's reappearance from the occultation, which lasted 25 minutes behind the planet, again five times, especially when meteorological observations confirmed the sky was completely free of clouds at the time of observation and at the observing place specifically. The measurements of the star's dimming before and after the occultation were then matched, and from the match of results it was confirmed that the planet contains rings, estimated at five rings.

In 1984 Dr. D.A. Allen from Australia was able to image the rings in infrared. When the spacecraft Voyager reached the planet in 1986, the pictures it transmitted showed that there are nine main rings surrounding the planet, some consisting of a huge number of ringlets circling the planet. The information confirmed that they are materials of low reflectivity and opaque. These nine rings circle the planet's equator from north to south! Because the planet orbits the Sun lying on its side, as we saw earlier.

The epsilon ring is the widest of these rings, and has two shepherd moons, Ophelia and Cordelia, whose diameters range between 20 and 25 km. Voyager 2 noticed that there is material forming a thin ring lying before the sixth ring and closer to the planet, extending at a distance of 37–39.5 thousand km from the planet. It also noticed the lambda ring, which is very faint and lies between the two outer rings delta and epsilon. The planet's rings in general are of little thickness, not exceeding several tens of metres. Some believe their material is dark, made of carbon, metals and some organic materials, because originally it was an asteroid that the planet captured and that broke up at the Roche limit, which led to the formation of these rings.

The moons of the planet Uranus:

After the discovery of the planet, studies concentrated on discovering its moons, and Herschel, the planet's discoverer, observed the planet's first two moons, Oberon and Titania, in 1787. The moon Oberon is distinguished by its brown colour, and its surface contains a number of craters distinguished by the presence of dark material in their middle, as if thrown from inside the moon. On its surface is a mountain 6 km high. The moon Titania is larger than Oberon and greater in mass, and its icy surface is full of craters that contain no dark materials. There are two large distinctive craters and a large rift 1,500 km long and 75 km wide. Lassell then followed in 1851 with the discovery of two other moons, Ariel and Umbriel. Umbriel has a dark surface and there is a bright spot on its surface, Wunda, as if a ring 140 km wide. The moon Ariel is full of craters like Umbriel. Then in 1948 Kuiper discovered the moon Miranda. These five moons are thus the ones discovered by telescope before the famous Voyager flight to the planet, which discovered ten other small moons when it passed in 1985–1986. The number of moons of the planet Uranus discovered up to 2006 became twenty-five. These moons orbit the planet from north to south just as the rings move in their orbit around the planet. They are roughly perpendicular to the planet's orbit around the Sun because of the planet's great axial tilt. They also face the Sun from the poles, as is the case with the planet.

The two shepherd moons of the epsilon ring work to keep the components of this ring from icy pieces. The first of the moons discovered by the spacecraft Voyager 2 is the moon Puck on 30/12/1985, inside the plane of the five known moons. Three clear craters were imaged on its surface, called Bogle, Lob and Butz, and as it came closer it began to give more information about the other small moons. We still lack much information about the moons of Uranus. The strangest of these moons is Miranda, so varied in terrain that it has been said to sum up all the terrain we know in the entire solar system. This moon, whose diameter does not exceed 500 km, contains craters of different ages, bright regions, icy highlands, valleys, depressions and much else of the terrain we know.

Neptune

Whoever follows the path of the spacecraft Voyager 2 on its historic journey of no return does not expect the precision of its instruments' results to reach their full capacity after the long years of travel in which it covered billions of kilometres. Twelve years after its launch in 1977, having covered a distance of nearly 4.5 billion km and being at a distance from which its radio signals need more than four hours to reach the Earth, from that far place on the edge of the solar system the spacecraft carrying humanity's message to the universe reached the eighth planet in distance from the Sun and the fourth of the gas giants and the last, in 1989, to send us everything its probes could measure and its cameras capture, so that our real knowledge of this giant planet Neptune began. The first thing that drew attention was the planet's wonderfully blue colour, which deservedly earned it the name of the god of the sea, Neptune, and this planet can be easily distinguished among the planets because of its colour, which the eye never mistakes. The planet Neptune is the first celestial body discovered by mathematical calculation and not by observation, which gave it a special place among the planets.

The story of the planet's discovery:

The story of the discovery of Neptune came after the path of the planet Uranus, discovered in 1781 by William Herschel, was determined and maps showing its path among the stars were made. When this path was studied a difference was noticed between its expected path on the maps by mathematical calculations and its true position when observed. This difference grew with time. The astronomer Alexis Bouvard was the first to prepare tables showing the planet's bewilderment and the irregularity of its path through his observations at the Paris Observatory, and he knew no explanation for it. Then came the astronomer Rev. T. J. Hussey, who was the first to suggest that the reason for the disturbance of Uranus's path might be the presence of a body outside Uranus's orbit affecting it, and he wrote to the then Astronomer Royal of Britain, Dr. Airy, who was working at Greenwich at the time, in 1835. But the reply was not encouraging. Even when Alexis's nephew, the astronomer Eugene Bouvard, sent him a letter in 1837 confirming that there was a clear error between the motion of the planet Uranus and the mathematical calculations of its position, saying: "Does this suggest the presence of an unknown force represented by a new body farther than Uranus, and this was my uncle's idea before me", he replied that he did not know the reason for these errors, and so he made the mistake a second time. Then F. W. Bessel in 1840 took the decision to calculate the distance of this new planet from us, he who in 1838 had determined the distance of the star 61 Cygni from us, but his illness prevented his progress in the work.

Then the English astronomer John Couch Adams at the University of Cambridge in September 1845 carried out studies through which he set the approximate distance and mass of that outer body supposed to affect the orbit of the planet Uranus, which was regarded as the unknown eighth planet, and expected its presence in the constellation Aquarius. His views and studies were met with rejection and lack of cooperation, so much so that Dr. Airy urged him not to continue with this. But Adams wrote to the professor of astronomy James Challis at the University of Cambridge to tell him the results he had reached through mathematical calculations, but because suitable maps were not available at that time and because his colleagues did not believe in his ability to achieve these results because of his youth, as he was twenty-six then, Adams did not succeed in achieving his aim. At the same time, separately and without knowledge of Adams's work, the French mathematician Urbain Jean Leverrier was carrying out calculations on the expected eighth planet and determined its mass and distance and sent his results to Dr. Airy, who could not observe this planet from the Greenwich observatory for lack of a suitable telescope, so the latter sent to Challis asking him to use his 11.75-inch reflecting telescope at Cambridge, and this request was dated 6/7/1846, but he did not have suitable, good maps of Aquarius to compare against.

Nor could he convince any of his French colleagues to observe so that this planet supposed to exist in Aquarius could be hunted down. He therefore sent a letter to the director of the Berlin Observatory, Johann Encke, who undertook to commission two German astronomers, Johann Galle and his student and assistant Heinrich d'Arrest, who began the search on 23/9/1846, and the first time Galle was able to locate the planet awaited for several years, with an error of less than one degree (about 55 arcminutes) from the place Leverrier had specified for him, who had no knowledge whatever of Adams's determination of the same planet in the same position. When John Herschel announced the news of the new discovery a dispute arose between the French and the British that ended with the glory being shared between Leverrier and Adams, although Galle was the first to observe the planet and practically locate its position on the page of the sky. It is amusing to learn that Challis of Cambridge had observed the position twice, the last on 12/8/1846, but did not bother to compare the maps, so he lost the honour of discovering the eighth planet and observing it first. It is interesting that Neptune, 233 years before its discovery, was next to Jupiter in the winter of 1612/1613, and it happened that Galileo observed Jupiter on 28/12/1612 and again on 22/1/1613 and recorded Neptune in his drawings, but thought it a star.

The planet Neptune:

The planet Neptune lies behind the wonderful planet Uranus, to be regarded as the eighth of the planets and the fourth of the gas giants. This planet's diameter is 49,520 km, about four times the Earth's diameter. Its volume is 72 times the Earth's volume. Its mass is 1 × 10²⁶ kg, so its mass is about 17 times the Earth's mass. Its density is 1.7 g/cm³, which makes it the densest of the gas giants, and this indicates it contains some heavy elements other than hydrogen and helium. More important is the presence of methane among its components at 3%, which makes its colour a wonderfully deep blue because methane gas has the ability to absorb the red and yellow colours of the spectrum and reflect the green and blue.
The reflectivity of its surface (albedo) is high, reaching about 84% of the amount of rays falling on it. But it cannot be seen with the naked eye because its magnitude reaches about 7.8 on average, a limit the human eye cannot observe. The planet is distinguished by an atmosphere full of stormy phenomena for a distant planet that sunlight reaches only at 1/900 of what reaches the Earth, and the most famous of these stormy phenomena is the Great Dark Spot, which circles the planet at a speed of 325 m/s and whose size equals an Earth-sized sphere. It resembles Jupiter's Great Red Spot in that it lies about 20° below the planet's equator and rotates counter-clockwise and in a direction from east to west, taking 18 hours and 20 minutes to complete one circuit of the planet. There is also the Small Dark Spot, or D2, which circles in the lower southern part of the planet and completes one circuit in 16 hours.
Inside it there are regions of bright clouds, and this spot rotates clockwise, also towards the west, and above it to the left there is a white cloud called Scooter, named for its enormous speed compared with the clouds we know, as it circles the planet in 16 hours and 50 minutes. These white clouds are made of crystals of methane ice and they sweep the planet's surface at an enormous speed of 2,200 km/h, which makes them among the most active and fastest winds on all the planets of the solar system, and not only that, the planet's atmosphere is distinguished by the retrograde motion of storms from east to west.

Structure of the planet Neptune:

The planet Neptune is flattened at its equator, which means its diameter between the north and south poles, 48,680 km, is less than its diameter at the equator, 49,520 km, by a difference of about 840 km. From the information transmitted by the spacecraft Voyager 2, scientists predicted a model of the planet's structure: they expected it to consist of a rocky core about the size of the Earth, dense in its material and composed of iron and silicates, above which lies a frozen layer of water in which ammonia has dissolved, forming an ionised layer whose ions are expected to be OH⁻, NH₄⁺ and H₃O⁺, and perhaps these ions explain the existence of the magnetic field, though it lacks ionised hydrogen. Above this layer lies a gaseous envelope composed of molecules of hydrogen, helium and, in greater proportion, methane.
We mentioned earlier that the planet's atmosphere is stormy, and perhaps there is a heat emission from inside the planet to explain the reason these storms occur. It was noticed that it radiates heat at 2.7 times more than it receives from the Sun's rays, and its surface temperature is about −214 °C, which is similar to the temperature of Uranus, which is about a billion km nearer the Sun! This source of heat is still a puzzle that perplexes scientists, but the prevailing opinion so far is that the planet Neptune has not yet cooled and this heat pouring from its interior is the initial heat of the planet's formation.

The orbit of the planet Neptune and its magnetic field:

This blue giant orbits the Sun at an average distance of 4.5 billion km, equal to 30.1 astronomical units, being at 29.8 astronomical units at perihelion and 30.4 astronomical units at aphelion, which shows that the eccentricity of its orbit is 0.01. It needs 165 years to complete its orbit around the Sun, that is, it completed its first full orbit since its discovery in 2011. The planet Neptune's axis is tilted about 30° from the perpendicular to the plane of the orbit. The planet has a weak magnetic field compared with the other giant planets, its strength being only about that of the Earth's magnetic field. Not only that, but the axis of its magnetic field is tilted 47° from the planet's axis of rotation, and this still needs explaining. The planet has a magnetic north pole pointing towards the geographic north, which means that if we placed an Earth compass on the planet it would point south, the opposite of what happens on Earth. There is still another point that distinguishes the planet Neptune from others, which is that the magnetic field completes its cycle in about 16 hours and 7 minutes while its gaseous envelope completes its cycle in 17 hours and 20 minutes, which means the rotation speed of the planet's visible gaseous envelope is slower than the cycle of its magnetic field.

The rings of the planet Neptune:

After Saturn's rings had been distinctive of it alone among the planets, the results of the spacecraft Voyager came to show that this characteristic exists in all the giant gas planets. The observations of two teams of scientists studying the occultation of a star behind the planet on 22/7/1984 came to confirm the existence of rings surrounding the planet. These two teams are a French team led by André Brahic of the European Southern Observatory and the second an American team led by William Hubbard 100 km south of the Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory, and the results of observations of both teams confirmed the existence of rings surrounding the planet at its equator, and the result was repeated in studying the occultation of another star behind the planet in 1985 by the same two teams too, and they even expected that this ring would not be continuous but fragmented in the form of dense arcs of material around the planet.

The definitive end of these studies was in the pictures of the spacecraft Voyager 2 in the period between 11 and 26 August 1989 as it passed round the planet Neptune, and the pictures showed four thin rings around the planet. The first ring, the farthest, is called 1989N1R and contains three arcs of material as if clumps of particles, which shows the ring's non-uniformity and shows the precision of the observations of the two Earth-based teams mentioned earlier. The second ring, 1989N2R, inside it, has a width of 15–30 km and consists of dusty material at 40–70%. Between them extends a ring distinguished by material spread to a width of 5,800 km, called Plateau, with the symbol 1989N4R. There is also a faint ring, the nearest to the planet, 15 km wide, with the symbol 1989N3R. All these rings orbit in a circular orbit around the plane of the planet's equator. Five rings were identified in another reference. In August 1991 the International Astronomical Union (IAU) decided to give the names of three nineteenth-century astronomers who had the merit of discovering the planet Neptune to its three bright rings, so the ring N1R was called Adams, the ring N2R Leverrier and the third ring N3R Galle, and they are, as we saw earlier, the ones who determined the planet's position and observed it.

The moons of the planet Neptune:

Before the arrival of the spacecraft Voyager 2, two moons were known for Neptune: the first is the moon Triton, discovered on 10/10/1846, 17 days after the discovery of the planet itself, by William Lassell. The second is the moon Nereid, discovered in 1949 by Gerard Kuiper, more than a hundred years after the planet's discovery. Both moons have strange orbits. As for the moon Nereid, its diameter is 340 km and its orbit is distinguished by a high eccentricity of 0.75, so that the moon is about 1.4 million km from the planet at perihelion and about 9.7 million km at aphelion, a great contrast not previously matched by any body in the entire solar system. The moon's orbit is tilted about 7.2° from the equator of the planet's orbit. The spacecraft Voyager discovered six other moons of the planet, small dark moons, and the terrain of the two moons Larissa and Proteus could be observed, two irregularly shaped moons orbiting in a circular orbit in the plane of the planet's equator. Pluto

Pluto is a dwarf planet that orbits the Sun in a remote, distant, cold orbit and lies in a region called the Kuiper belt, which contains many icy bodies like it. Since its discovery in 1930 everyone counted Pluto as the ninth planet of the solar system, but because of its small size and its irregular, highly elongated orbit, the idea was put forward of removing this planet from the rank of planets and including it in the group of icy Kuiper bodies that resemble it more in characteristics. Indeed the International Astronomical Union held a meeting in the city of Prague in August 2006 that included a number of decisions, among them redefining the planet, on which was based the removal of Pluto from the position of a major planet in the solar system and its transfer to the list of dwarf planets. We still know very, very little about it. Before speaking of its discovery let us speak of the latest decision of the International Astronomical Union.

After the discovery of the planet Uranus in 1781 and calculations to learn the reason for the fault in the planet's orbit, a planet in an orbit beyond it was predicted to explain the disturbance, and indeed through astronomical calculations the planet Neptune was discovered in 1846, and after that studies were active to search for a new planet outside Neptune's orbit. Percival Lowell was one of the wealthy men who were dazzled by the idea of a Martian civilisation, as we read earlier, and one of those possessed by an obsession with searching for the ninth planet, and he recommended a systematic photographic programme to survey the sky. In 1905 photographic observations were carried out from the Lowell Observatory in Arizona and from Mount Wilson Observatory in California to search for this unknown planet. In 1914 an advanced telescope was set up at the Lowell Observatory, which he had built for himself and which is still working to this day, and by means of this telescope the serious search for the planet began. But Lowell died in 1916 before the planet was discovered. In 1919 Milton Humason took pictures of the sky as Pickering had specified for him, but unfortunately he could not discern the presence of a planet in these plates, although it did actually appear on a number of the photographic plates; the first reason was that he thought there was an error in the photographic plates, and when the photography was repeated the planet appeared in the same place, so it looked to him like the stars. In 1929 Clyde William Tombaugh began his search for the planet from a new observatory built especially for the purpose of searching for the ninth planet, by means of which he could photograph a wide field of the sky. Only one year earlier Tombaugh had been living on a small farm in Kansas and observing with a small telescope he had made himself. After a gruelling year of searching, studying and photographing, Tombaugh found the sought planet on 18 February 1930 on plates taken at the beginning of 1930, and the discovery was announced on 13 March 1930, on the anniversary of the birth of Lowell, who had the idea of searching for this planet. The planet was given the name Pluto and symbolised by the symbol ♇, the two letters P and L, representing the initials of Percival Lowell's name. From here we see that the planet was not the result of precise mathematical calculations but the fruit of research and organised, gruelling effort. Tombaugh discovered the planet Pluto in a place only five degrees from the place that both Lowell and Pickering had predicted; Pluto lay in the constellation Gemini, the same constellation in which Herschel discovered the planet Uranus in 1781. The discovery of Pluto was disappointing compared with the victory achieved by the discovery of Neptune, because its mass and size were far too small to affect the orbital motion of both Uranus and Neptune, as the mass of this new planet had been expected to be more than ten times the Earth's mass. The discovery of the planet Pluto is thus considered the greatest scientific detective effort in history because of its low apparent brightness, with about twenty million stars sharing this brightness with it, and the lack of good telescopes able to distinguish it was among the most important reasons for the delay in its discovery.

Comets

Comets are irregularly shaped celestial bodies made of masses of ice, rock and gases, and the astronomer Fred Whipple in 1950 proposed a description indicating that comets are "dirty snowballs", because they are mostly composed of ice mixed with gases and some dust and rock. They differ in their properties from the planets: apart from their irregular shapes compared with the planets, which are generally spherical, their orbits are highly elongated, so that their position relative to the Sun varies enormously in terms of nearness and farness compared with the elliptical or nearly circular orbits of the planets.

It has been agreed to divide comets into two main categories according to the length of each one's cycle around the Sun, and these two categories are:

1- Long-period comets, whose period of revolution around the Sun takes more than 200 years.

2- Short-period comets, whose period of revolution around the Sun takes less than 200 years. Halley's Comet, which completes one revolution around the Sun in 76 years, is of this type. Meanwhile the famous comet Hale-Bopp, which passed through Jordan's sky in 1997 and of which many wonderful pictures were taken, some of which gained worldwide fame (among them pictures by our colleague Mohammad Odeh of the Jordanian Astronomical Society), is of the first, long-period type, completing one revolution around the Sun in 2,400 years.

Comets are not seen when they are in their orbit far from the Sun, because they are opaque bodies that do not shine by themselves, but as they approach the Sun the material of this dark mass begins to evaporate, break up and sublimate. This material is swept behind the comet's body by the solar wind, forming a very long tail extending behind the comet's nucleus, and this material reflects the sunlight falling on it so that we see it.

The direction of this tail is always away from the Sun, and when the comet reaches this stage its concept takes shape as we imagine it, with a nucleus, a coma surrounding the nucleus and a tail that may extend for millions of kilometres. The parts of the comet are clear in pictures of Comet West, which appeared in 1975. As the comet moves on and departs from the Sun, the tail also begins to disappear gradually. A comet may form more than one tail, and these two tails are:



The ion (gas) tail, Plasma Tail:

This tail is made of ionised gases and electrons and its colour tends to blue because of the ionisation of carbon monoxide, which gives the sharp blue colour as shown in the picture. This tail extends in a straight line to very great distances, approaching an astronomical unit, that is, equal to 150 million km behind the comet's nucleus in the direction opposite the Sun.

The dust tail, Dust Tail:

This tail represents dust grains that were swept by the solar wind behind the comet, so its colour tends to white or yellow. This dust tail is distinguished by being broader and shorter than the ion tail and diverging from it in an arc, because dust particles are heavier than ionised gas.

These dust particles remain behind the comet, forming a stream of grains, dust, ice and other materials that sublimated or evaporated because of the comet's approach to the Sun, and this stream of particles travels around the Sun in the comet's own orbit. On each visit near the Sun the comet releases much gas and dust into its orbit. With the passage of time, after several visits, this material spreads in the comet's own orbit and passes through several stages of development ending in a broad orbit of low density. The Earth crosses this orbit at least once during its motion round the Sun in its orbit. It may cross through it twice. This crossing process is what generates the phenomenon of meteor showers and is linked with them, as we will see later.

The Sun

The Sun is a star of medium size and temperature in the Milky Way galaxy. The Sun is considered the central star to which our system of planets, including the Earth, belongs, and the average distance of the Earth's centre from the Sun's centre is one astronomical unit, about 150 million km. The Earth at this average distance receives a suitable amount of energy and heat that helps maintain the survival of life on Earth.

The Sun, which belongs to spectral class G of medium-sized stars, has a surface temperature of about 6,000 kelvin and its colour is yellow.
